# Camera Games: full details

Camera games for children with hemiplegia, played by moving in front of a webcam. Opening the hand is what most of them practise, and in Giraffe's Garden, turning it over, in Giraffe's Goal, holding it up with the wrist straight, in Flap, Little Chick!, bending the wrists back, with both hands, and in Arrow Meadow, the two hands doing different jobs at once. Mighty Lift is raising an arm, with the whole-body tracker, Moto Mountain leaning the body to each side and holding it there, Rooftop Rope standing on one leg and holding the other foot up, and Gobble and Grow is opening the mouth wide, the first game played with the face. Frost Arena is the odd one out: a shooter for grown-up players, played with both hands at once, each used like a thumb on the glass of a phone. The right hand is the default, and the left can be chosen instead under the gear icon.

They're played at <https://hemi.infoicy.com> in Google Chrome, on a computer with a webcam, or from this folder (see [Run it](#run-it)). The camera picture never leaves the computer, and once loaded they work offline, apart from playing with friends. Each game has ten levels, and a grown-up can set how open the hand has to be, how far to reach and how big a move has to be.

- **High Five Racers:** the car only drives while the hand is open. Steering over to a giant hand at the roadside high fives it for a boost, and keeping the hand open fills up turbo. Nobody can crash or lose: if the hand closes, the car coasts to a stop and waits. See [below](#high-five-racers).
- **Balloon Pop:** balloons float up and drift around, and the open hand pops them. A closed hand just bumps them. Missing a balloon costs nothing. A grown-up can turn on a maths challenge, where the wrong answer burns the glove.
- **Mighty Moves:** a fighting game against the computer, on their own or side by side with a friend, or against a friend, each on their own computer, played with the whole body. The coach shows concrete moves to do, like a clap, a squat, a jump or a hand circle, and only those can win. Ordinary punches and kicks are small hits. See [below](#mighty-moves).
- **Leap Trail:** a fast run down a country path in 3D, on your own or with friends, jumping over what's in the way and stepping from side to side between three lanes to grab lines of stars, with the child's real body. See [below](#leap-trail).
- **Sky Wings:** a dogfight over a 3D island, with both arms as wings. Tipping them banks and turns the plane, and raising or lowering them flies it up and down. Sky pirates hunt the child and the child hunts them: the guns fire by themselves at any plane inside the ring, until they've all been dropped.
- **Robot Rescue:** robots have caught the friends from the other games, and the child rides a little tram through a seaside town to free them. An open hand fires at whatever is in the crosshair, and a closed hand raises a shield and reloads. See [below](#robot-rescue).
- **Tank Patrol:** robot tanks are rolling into Giraffe's valley, and he rides along with his long neck sticking out of the hatch. With one hand: closing it drives the tank to where it points, and opening it fires at any robot tank under the crosshair that the gun can reach. See [below](#tank-patrol).
- **Cannon Bay:** a sea battle around the 3D island, with one hand. Moving it from side to side turns the ship's wheel, and opening it raises the sails. The cannons fire by themselves at any pirate ship in a firing zone. See [below](#cannon-bay).
- **Giraffe's Long Walk Home:** The giraffe is lost and hungry. The child closes a hand on fruit, carries it across to his mouth and opens the hand to feed him, and each stop fed takes him closer to his family.
- **Spider Catcher:** Giraffe's little spider friends have run off all over the garden, scuttling about and swinging on their threads. The child opens a hand by one and closes it to pick it up, carries it across to its web and opens the hand to put it home. See [below](#spider-catcher).
- **Giraffe's Smoothie Stand:** at the Harvest Fair, each customer orders a smoothie, and the child slices what they ordered with a swipe of the open hand. Bad fruit makes the glove sticky until it has been held open.
- **The Lighthouse Keeper:** mud from a storm has covered the windows and ships are lost at sea. Wiping a window clean with an open hand lets the light out, and brings a ship home. On their own or with friends, all wiping the same windows. See [below](#playing-the-lighthouse-keeper-with-friends).
- **Bounce Back:** tennis with a big, slow, floaty ball, against the computer, or with a friend: together, keeping one rally going as a team, or against each other. The open hand is the racket, and only an open hand sends the ball back over the net. Resting never loses a point. See [below](#bounce-back).
- **Giraffe's Maze:** Giraffe's friends are lost in a garden maze. The child's open hand leads him along the paths to each of them, and each friend found follows him into the next maze. A closed hand, or one that can't be seen, just leaves him waiting. On their own, or racing friends out of the same mazes. See [below](#giraffes-maze).
- **Giraffe's Garden:** Giraffe's garden has dried up in the sun. The open hand holds his watering can, and turning the hand over tips it, so the water pours onto the plant below, which grows and flowers. Turning the hand over is turning the forearm, the hardest movement of all with hemiplegia. On their own or with friends, all watering the same garden. See [below](#giraffes-garden).
- **Giraffe's Goal:** the cup final has gone to penalties, and Giraffe's team needs a goalkeeper. A kicker shoots once the child's hand is open and held up like a high five, a ring shows where the ball is coming, and the glove there saves it. An open hand with its wrist dropped doesn't count, and the balls go far out to the side and across the body on purpose. See [below](#giraffes-goal).
- **Frost Arena:** a team shooter for grown-up players, in a snowy yard, seen through the player's own eyes: blue, with Giraffe on it, against the computer's red. It's played with two hands in the air the way a shooter is played with two thumbs on a phone: a hand that opens is a thumb put down, and dragged from there, one hand to walk, sidestep, back away and sprint, and the other to look round. A closed hand is a thumb lifted, and a thumb held up fires on one hand and changes the magazine on the other. Up to four friends can play it together, or against each other, each on their own computer. See [below](#frost-arena).
- **Arrow Meadow:** archery at the meadow fair, with both hands. One hand holds Giraffe's bow, and how much higher it is than the other tilts the bow. The hand in play holds the string by being closed, or just relaxed, pulling the two apart draws the bow, and opening that hand lets the arrow fly, along the dotted line that showed where it would go. A miss costs nothing. Up to four friends can play it together, or against each other, each on their own computer. See [below](#arrow-meadow).
- **Giraffe's Puzzles:** Giraffe's picture fell off the wall and broke into pieces. An open hand over a piece picks it up, and the piece goes with the hand for as long as it stays open, to its place in the picture, where it snaps in. A hand that closes or rests just leaves the piece where it is, so nothing has to be gripped. On their own or with friends: together, all mending the same picture, or against each other, each with a copy of it. See [below](#giraffes-puzzles).
- **Flap, Little Chick!:** a little chick has to get home to the hen house along a farm track, with a fox trotting along behind. The child's two hands are its wings: each time a hand comes up from hanging forwards, bent back at the wrist like a high five, that wing flaps, and a flap in good time carries the chick over what's in the way. One hand alone is a hop, and both together go over a fence. The fox can catch a chick that stands too long or tumbles too often, and then it has to start again: a grown-up can have him never catch anyone, with the chick waiting for as long as it takes. Up to four friends can fly home together, as one flock, or race each other, each on their own computer. See [below](#flap-little-chick).
- **Mighty Lift:** someone is stuck under something heavy, and the child lifts it off them by raising an arm: the weight goes as high as the arm does, and held up for a moment, the friend scrambles free. Each arm's line is measured against its own reach, and the line comes down to meet a child who's trying. On their own, or with friends, lifting one big weight together or each freeing their own. See [below](#mighty-lift).
- **Gobble and Grow:** the child is a tiny creature that grows by eating, from a bacterium to a galaxy, one world to each level. It's played with the mouth: opening it wide gobbles up whatever small thing is in reach, and shutting it munches. Opening the mouth wide and closing it again is hard for children with one weaker side of the face. When a world is won, the view pulls slowly back to the next, with a picture of how much the child grew. On their own, or with up to four friends, together or against each other. See [below](#gobble-and-grow).
- **Giraffe's Paint Box:** Giraffe is painting pictures for the party, and the child colours them in. The open hand is the brush: paint goes down where it moves, and never over the lines, and dipping it in a paint pot takes that paint. A closed hand is the brush lifted. On their own, or with up to three friends, all painting one picture together or each their own, to see who finishes first. See [below](#giraffes-paint-box).
- **Moto Mountain:** a motorbike race up a winding mountain road, steered by leaning the body to the side. In a bend the bike drifts to the outside, and leaning into the bend, a little for a gentle one and further for a tight one, held until the road straightens, keeps it on the road. It's a race against other riders, over steep hills that throw the bike into the air, with ramps, hay bales in the way and a river to jump, and a lean held in the air is a trick. A whole lean is measured against the child's own lean to each side, and the grass beside the road only slows the bike. A grown-up can choose the road alone. On their own, or with up to three friends, riding up together as one convoy or racing to the top. See [below](#moto-mountain).
- **Rooftop Rope:** ropes are strung between rooftops high over a city, and the child stands on one leg to cross them. While one foot is held off the floor, the walker rolls along the rope on a wheel, standing on it on one foot, faster the higher the foot, and with both feet down it wobbles and in the end drops slowly into a net and starts that rope again. Rooftops are for resting on, for as long as the child likes. A whole lift is measured against the child's own lift with each foot. On their own, or with up to three friends, crossing side by side or racing to the last rooftop. See [below](#rooftop-rope).

Every game's grown-ups' panel, under the gear icon, has a way back to all the games. On the page for choosing a game, each game's card plays a film of it being played, with just its name, and pointing at a card shows a line about the game. With reduced motion turned on in the computer's settings, a film only plays while its card is pointed at.

**Finding a game.** At the top of that page, typing in the search box finds games by their name, what they're about or what kind of game they are, so "shoot", "tennis" or "maths" each find the right ones, whatever the capitals or accents. The filters button at the end of the search box opens a panel for choosing the kind of game (shooting; racing, running and flying; sports; fighting; puzzles and thinking; stories and missions; catching and popping), what it's played with (one hand, both arms or the whole body), and whether it can be played sitting down or with friends. Two kinds chosen show games of either kind, and the same goes for what they're played with, while sitting down and with friends each have to fit. Leap Trail counts as playable sitting down, by raising an open hand under **How to get over things**. Once some games are left out, a line above the cards says how many are showing, with **Show all games** to clear the search and filters, and when nothing fits, a box says so, with the same button. A card that's hidden stops its film, and doesn't fetch it until it's back. The search and filters are kept in the page's address, so going back to the page from a game brings them back, while **All games** in a game always shows every game.

## Run it

You need Google Chrome, Python 3 and a webcam. Chrome is the only browser these games target. Nothing else to install.

```
cd camera-games.infoicy.com
npm start
```

Then open <http://localhost:8642> in Chrome and choose a game. Chrome only allows the camera on `localhost` or HTTPS, so opening `index.html` directly won't work. To play with friends from here, also run `npm run race-server`, which needs Node.

**To add a game,** make a folder in `games/` with an `index.html` (copy one) and a `main.js`. That file gives `GameSession` the game's words and settings, plus an object with a few methods: `prepare`, `begin`, `update`, `idle`, `render`, `prompt` and `summary`. They're described at the top of [core/session.js](core/session.js), and [games/balloon-pop/main.js](games/balloon-pop/main.js) is a short example. Then add a card to the root `index.html`. Hand tracking, the hand check, voice, pausing and the panel all come with it.

**A card's tags,** for the search and filters, go on its first line. `data-tags` lists the filters that find it: at least one kind of game (`shooting`, `racing`, `sports`, `fighting`, `puzzles`, `stories` or `catching`), what it's played with (`hand`, `arms` or `body`), and `sitting` if it can be played sitting down. `data-words` adds any other words to find it by, such as `tennis` for Bounce Back. A maze played with one hand would be `data-tags="puzzles hand sitting" data-words="maze"`. A game played with friends has the friends badge on its card, and the badge is what puts it under **With friends**. The filters themselves are the checkboxes in the page's filters panel, each with the tag it finds as its value and more words that find it, so a new kind of game is one more of those. The hub's tests check that every card has a kind and what it's played with, and no tag the filters don't know.

**To add a whole-body game,** extend `BodyGame` in [core/body/game.js](core/body/game.js), whose top lists what a game has to fill in, and call `mountBodyShell` for the screens. [games/leap-trail/main.js](games/leap-trail/main.js) is an example. The camera, the check that it can see the child, the ten characters and the panel all come with it.

## How "open" is decided

All of this is in [core/hands.js](core/hands.js).

Each finger scores 0 (curled) to 1 (straight) from two measurements that must agree: how much it bends along its length, and how far its tip reaches from the wrist. The thumb scores on how far its tip is from the palm. Overall openness then weighs the weakest finger as much as the average, and gives the thumb a full third, so one curled finger or a thumb tucked into the palm costs a lot.

What the real model scored on real photos:

| Pose | Openness |
|---|---|
| Fist | 0.00 |
| Pointing | 0.08 |
| Peace sign | 0.17 |
| Thumbs up | 0.23 |
| Loosely open, curved fingers | 0.55–0.73 |
| Wide open | 0.91–1.00 |

**The hand sets its own line.** The hand check at the start measures its best opening, and by default it must reach 80% of that. An affected hand that can't open fully can still play, and the grown-ups' panel can move the line.

**A fist can never pass.** However low the settings go, the line never drops below 0.35. Across rotation, dim light and blur, a fist's score stayed below 0.2. A hand that can't reach 0.35 can't play, which with a tucked thumb means fingers about half straight. To change that for a child who needs it, edit `floor` in `OPEN_RULES`.

**Anything uncertain counts as not open:** a hand out of view, cut off by the edge of the picture, too far away, overlapped by the other hand (so one hand can't hold the other open), or a stalled tracker.

The numbers worth tuning with a therapist are `OPEN_RULES` at the top of `hands.js`.

## Choosing the hand

Choose **Hand to play with** under the gear icon. It's remembered across the games. The other hand is ignored, so it can rest, close or leave the picture, except for one extra in High Five Racers, below, which it can never be punished for missing. It still can't overlap the hand in play, so one hand can't hold the other open. In High Five Racers, moving the hand side to side steers, measured from where it started in the hand check.

**How it tells left from right.** It uses the tracker's own left or right label, because the hand could be anywhere in the picture. The label swapped correctly on every mirrored photo, at angles up to 60° and including fists, with confidence never below 0.88. It also named both hands correctly in a photo with crossed arms. When the tracker is less than 70% sure, the hand's side of the mirror decides instead, so a child doesn't get stuck being asked for a hand they're showing. If the tracker is sure it's seeing the other hand, that hand doesn't count, and the panel says "Only sees the other hand".

## Hand shapes

Under the gear icon, **Hand shapes** chooses what makes a game's active move, named for what it does there: *Drive* in High Five Racers, *Fire* in Robot Rescue and Tank Patrol, *Pop* in Balloon Pop. It can be:

- **Open hand**, as the games have always been, which is how every game starts.
- **Pointing** (the index finger straight and the other three curled), **Two fingers**, **Three fingers** or **Thumb up**.
- **Choose fingers**: any fingers straight, and the rest curled.

The passive move is always the closed hand: any hand that isn't making the active move's shape, as a resting hand does. With pointing to drive, an open hand or a fist slows the car down. A child who can't open the whole hand may well manage pointing, and moving one finger at a time is good practice too. Each game keeps its own choice, so pointing can drive in High Five Racers while Balloon Pop still pops with an open hand.

**How a shape is told apart.** It's in [core/shapes.js](core/shapes.js). A finger counts as straight from a score of 0.55 and curled up to 0.35, with room for a finger that can't straighten all the way. A shape is as sure as its least sure finger, it counts once it's 60% sure for a fifth of a second, and it stays made until it's under 40% sure, so it doesn't flicker. On the real photos, each of pointing, a peace sign and a thumbs up makes its own shape and none of the others, and an open hand and a fist make none of them. The thumb is left out of pointing, two and three fingers, as thumbs sit every which way. As always, a hand out of view, at the edge of the picture, too far away or overlapping the other hand doesn't count.

The hand check asks for the shape, and the picture in the corner shows it. The prompts ask for it in its own words, like "Point with one finger!", and show it. "How open counts as open" is only for the open hand, so the panel puts it away while the move is a shape. In High Five Racers, the other hand's fist and then open hand for flying stays as it is.

**One picker for every game** ([core/mapping-ui.js](core/mapping-ui.js), with its rules in [core/mapping.js](core/mapping.js)). The panel shows a row for each thing a game can be told to do with a hand, and under it a chip for everything it can be given to, each with a little picture of the hand making it. Most games have the one row, for their open move. A game that gives several jobs to its hands lists a row for each, and they go in the same picker, look the same and are kept the same way, with the game's hand shapes: Frost Arena has five, what each hand drags with and what fires, changes the magazine and sprints. A row in a game played with both hands can say which hand does it, and can offer things that aren't a hand shape, like nothing at all. Two things on the same hand can't be given to the same shape, so a chip that's taken is greyed out, and says what has it. Moved to the other hand, a row keeps its shape if that's free there, and takes the first free one if not. Something kept that a row doesn't offer any more is what the row is out of the box.

Every hand shape any game offers is named and drawn in one place, [core/shapes.js](core/shapes.js): the ones above, and a pinch of the thumb against each finger and the first finger curled, which only Frost Arena offers and reads for itself. So a new shape, or a new look for the chips, is a change in one place for every game. To give a game more rows, pass `mapping` to `GameSession`, as [games/frost-arena/main.js](games/frost-arena/main.js) does, and read what's chosen from `session.given`.

## Levels

Every game has ten levels, and each is harder than the one before. Winning a game moves it on to the next level, up to level 10. A game that's lost comes round again at the same level, and the big button on its finish card says **Try again**. Each game remembers its own level. It's on the title card, under the countdown's numbers and on the finish card, with a dot for each level. A grown-up can choose any level under the gear icon, and the new level starts with the next game.

Level 1 plays the way each game always has. After that, levels bring more going on at once and a quicker pace, and every level from 2 brings something new to see and play with, such as a new kind of enemy or obstacle, a new kind of fire, or prizes to pick up. What's new is named on the title card and under the countdown, and the finish card shows what the next level brings. Missing a prize never costs anything. What each game changes is in its own section below, and its numbers are in its rules file.

**Games can be lost.** To begin with, nothing in these games could be lost: whatever chased the child stopped short, a helper always stepped in, and every finished game moved the level on. On 14 November 2026 a grown-up who had played them asked for them to be more of a challenge: "make sure fox can catch, right now it just stops", and "make them more challenging in general". So as a game comes now, what threatens in it can win: the fox can catch the chick, a race can be lost, a clock can run out. Each game's section says what can be lost in it. Each also names the gentle setting, under the gear icon, that brings back the game as it was, where nothing can be lost, for a child or a therapist who needs that.

**A lost game is short, and kind.** It ends there, with a card that says what happened in the game's own story and the button to try again. It isn't cheered: there's no fanfare, no clapping and no confetti. The voice says the game's own line for it, or, where a game has none, "So close! Try again!".

**What a level never changes:** anything a grown-up or therapist chose. That's the hand in play, how open a hand has to be, how far there is to reach, how big a movement has to be, a gentle setting, help like a car that steers itself, and how long a game was set to be. With nobody in the picture, or no hand to see, a game waits, and so does whatever threatens in it: a child who walks away isn't caught for it. Every level-10 game is also tested to make sure nothing flashes. How much harder level 10 is than level 1 is in each game's section. It's no longer held to 70% of level 1's time to react, as it was when nothing could be lost.

**Settings saved before the games changed.** A game's settings are saved all together, in this browser. So a child who had played before 14 November 2026 has the way each game came then saved, though nobody chose it. Where that way was changed, what was saved is dropped, once, so that the game comes as it does now. A grown-up who really had chosen the old way has to choose it again, under the gear icon. Which settings these are is in `OLD_DEFAULTS` in `core/settings.js`.

## Choices made for these children

- **The hand check never asks for a fist,** since making one is the opposite of what's being practised.
- **Nothing flashes.** Seizures are more common in children with hemiplegia, so there are no strobes or full-screen effects. Road markings are long and low contrast, and a pop is a small burst of colour.
- **Prompts are spoken and point at the hand,** for children who can't read yet or are unsure of left and right. Each is said twice at most, and only when nothing else is being said.
- **Racing:** steering is measured from where the hand starts, so it works wherever an affected arm is comfortable. The car can also steer itself, so a child can work on opening alone. After 15 seconds with the hand closed, the game says resting is fine.
- **Balloons:** only an open hand pops, so a child can't score with a fist. The hand can rest freely between balloons, and is only asked to open when it bumps one.
- **Balloons also help with reach:** the camera's central area fills the whole screen, so less arm movement covers more of it. Under **Reach help**, balloons drift towards a nearby open hand, and a friend's basket comes with its balloons, all of it together. Balloon speed and how many to pop can be set too.
- **Lots to do, and little to listen to.** No game stops for talking. Characters and the coach say one short thing, praise is only now and then, and there's plenty on the screen at once. The hand check takes two seconds, and the countdown about two more.
- **Little films show what to do,** rather than leaving it to words: a hand opening during the hand check, a hand going from side to side to steer, and an open hand popping a balloon.

### The maths challenge in Balloon Pop

It's off unless a grown-up chooses it under **Maths challenge**: adding up to 10, adding and taking away up to 20, or times tables. A question is shown big on the screen, the voice says "Pop the right answer!", and balloons with answers on them come up and wait, for as long as it takes. A child reaching for one answer may have to pass another, so an open hand has to stay on a balloon for about half a second to choose it, with a ring closing around it to show that's happening.

The right answer pops, and the next question comes. The wrong one goes up in smoke and burns the glove: it turns black with soot and can't pop anything until the hand has been held open to cool it, which takes about a second and a half of being open, and a fist doesn't cool it. That makes the penalty the exercise itself. No right answers are taken away. The burn is cartoon soot, and nothing flashes. The summary counts the too-hot balloons as well as the right ones.

**Levels in Balloon Pop.** Level 1 is the game as described above. Each level brings balloons a little faster and a little more often, with more up at once and more of them drifting wide, and every level from 2 brings something new, which stays for the levels after it:

| Level | What's new |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Present balloons.** Popping one drops its present on a little parachute, and catching it gives a sweet. |
| 3 | **The bubble glove.** Now and then a soap bubble floats up with a hand in it. Popping it puts a see-through bubble round the glove for 8 seconds, which pops and catches from further away. |
| 4 | **Star balloons,** which only pop once the open hand has stayed on them for about a third of a second, with a ring closing round them. Each drops a gold star. |
| 5 | **Pairs** tied together, and popping both drops a gem. The game moves to the seaside. |
| 6 | **Friends in baskets:** Giraffe first, then Mochi, Frost and Truffle float up in a basket under three balloons. Each one popped slows the basket, and the third brings it gently down on a parachute, leaving a heart. |
| 7 | **The magnet,** a power-up that draws prizes and nearby balloons to the hand for 8 seconds, and a gentle breeze that pushes every balloon slowly one way and back. |
| 8 | **Rainbow balloons,** whose pop spreads to up to six balloons round them, one after another. The game moves to the Harvest Fair. |
| 9 | **The bluebird,** a power-up that stays by the hand for 8 seconds and pops a balloon near it about once a second. |
| 10 | **Big balloons,** which pop into three ordinary ones that drift apart from where it was. |

Prizes fall slowly, taking about ten seconds to come down the screen, drift towards a hand that's near them, and are caught just by the glove coming within about a palm and a half, open or closed. One that isn't caught fades on the hills, and missing it costs nothing. They're counted on the screen from level 2, and on the finish card by kind. A power-up's bubble pops like a balloon but doesn't count as one, and a ring round the glove shows its time running out. Its time only runs while the hand is open, so resting never wastes it, and the bluebird only pops balloons while the hand is open. The magnet draws a friend's basket in too. Power-ups and friends in baskets only come during play, never behind the title or finish card, and when a game ends the bluebird flies off. The first time each new thing comes along in a game, it has a small label beside it for two seconds, such as "Pop it for a present!", in the language the voice speaks, and nothing new is said aloud. Even at level 10 each balloon is on the screen for three quarters of the time it was at level 1, and no more than twice as many come along a minute, counting the three a big balloon pops into and a basket's balloons. How many to pop, the speed chosen, reach help and the hand stay as a grown-up set them. With the maths challenge on, the balloons with answers are as before, and levels 4 and 8 each add one more answer to choose from, up to five; the new kinds of balloon, the prizes and the power-ups don't come during it, and switching it on stops the bluebird and a rainbow's spreading pop. The cards then only list what comes with it: one more answer where a level really adds one (levels 4 and 8 for adding up to 10, level 4 for the others, as five is the most), and a new place. Nothing new flashes: the bubble is see-through, prizes turn slowly, and the tally only grows a little and settles when a prize lands in it. Measured at levels 1, 6 and 10, with prizes, power-ups, baskets and rainbows on the screen, brightness changed by less than 3.5 out of 255 from one frame to the next. The numbers are in `balloonLevel` in `rules.js`.

### Playing Balloon Pop with friends

Up to 4 children can play one game together, each on their own computer with their own camera, with the same balloons and the same sums, to see who pops the most, or with the maths challenge, who gets the most right.

- **Making and joining:** under **Play with a friend** on the title card, a grown-up presses **Make a game** and tells their friend the 3-letter code, or sends the link. The friend types the code, or opens the link, and is in.
- **Starting:** everyone presses **Let’s pop!**. After the hand check, each waits until everyone is ready, and then all count down together. Whoever made the game can press **Start now**, and anyone can play on their own.
- **The time:** everyone plays for the same time instead of to a goal: a minute for the host's 20 balloons, two for 40 and three for 60. A clock shows what's left.
- **One game for everyone:** the host's game runs the balloons and the sums, and everyone sees the same ones, where they are on the host's screen. Whoever's hand gets to a balloon first has it, and it's gone for everyone: the server decides, as each touch reaches it. Each sum is the same for everyone, and once anyone has the right answer, everyone gets the next. A wrong answer burns only that child's glove, and goes for everyone.
- **Fair for everyone:** with friends, the balloons are plain ones, without the presents, stars and power-ups the levels add, at the host's speed. They come up right across the screen, as each child may play with a different hand, and none drift towards a hand, as reach help would make a balloon one child's before anyone else could get to it.
- **Scores:** everyone's score shows as they play, the most first. The finish card says who came where, and equal scores share a place.
- **Friends' hands:** each friend's glove shows in light grey, with their name over it, where their hand is on their own screen, copying it finger by finger. It's only there to see, and pops nothing.
- **Levels and settings:** a game with friends doesn't move anyone's own level on, and nobody's settings change.

Only the name typed, the character from the other games, the score, where the glove is and which balloons it got to go to friends, and from the host's game, where the balloons are and the sum. Never anything from the camera.

## High Five Racers

A race round a lap with grandstands, a village, a tunnel, a farm, a lake, a forest, Giraffe's family and the Harvest Fair. Giraffe drives unless a grown-up picks someone else under **Who drives**, and other characters from the games drive the other cars. They stay close, so there's always someone to pass, and they ease off on the last stretch.

**High fives.** Giant open hands stand on alternating sides of the road, and the stars lead over to each one. Reaching the hand across moves the car across the road. A big hand goes green once the car is lined up, and only a hand read as open slaps it, for a burst of shapes and a boost. A closed or unreadable hand gets a soft pat and no boost.

**Turbo.** Keeping the hand open fills the turbo meter, in about 4 seconds of driving, and when it's full turbo goes by itself for 3 seconds, with flames, more speed and a wider view. Then the meter fills again. It doesn't matter how wide the hand is, only that it counts as open, so a hand that can't open far gets it as often as any other. Closing the hand pauses it: turbo stops, and nothing that was filled is lost.

**Flying, with the other hand.** Making a fist with the other hand and opening it again makes the car take off, with turbo: about 6 m up and 3 seconds in the air, or a low hop with reduced motion, and "Fly!" pops up. It works any time the car is moving. The fist has to be a real one, in view: a hand cut off by the edge of the picture, too far away or touching the other one doesn't count as a fist. The hand in play still has to be open for the car to go. The flight stays under the banners, footbridges and the tunnel's roof, and flying high over a big hand is neither a high five nor a miss.

**For children with hemiplegia:**

- **How far to reach** can be set, and a side that keeps being missed brings its hands closer, each side on its own. **Car steers itself** still does all of the steering.
- **Resting is always fine.** Closing the hand brings up the brake lights and the car coasts to a stop. Everyone waits, and resting costs no places.
- **The summary counts high fives to each side.**
- **Nothing flashes.** Measured at levels 1, 6 and 10 through slaps, turbo, the tunnel, a flight and a whole lap, and through the sunset, the shower, the power-ups and the giant high ten, brightness changed by less than 5% from one frame to the next. The tunnel is as bright as outdoors, and anything repeating along the road goes past fewer than three times a second.
- **On a slow computer** it gives up sharpness, then shadows, then flowers and grass, then bushes, rocks, distant things and half the crowd.

**Turbo and flying are shown, not said.** The meter shows an open hand and "Keep your hand open!", and "Turbo!" with flames when it's on. Each time turbo runs out, a little film by the meter shows the other hand, on its own side as in a mirror, making a fist and opening, and the car taking off: the first three times each race, and after that only until the car has flown.

**Levels.** Level 1 is the race described above. Level by level the other cars race a little harder, and every level from 2 brings something new, which stays for the levels after it:

| Level | What's new |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Coins.** A big hand slapped, a car passed and the tractor passed each spill a few coins onto the road ahead, which drift over to the car. |
| 3 | **The magnet,** a power-up on a trail of stars, which for 7 seconds pulls in the stars and coins a little way ahead from right across the road. **Two big hands in a row** on one side. |
| 4 | **A tractor** comes out of the farm and trundles along the shoulder, off the road, on the side away from the next big hand, so nothing can run into it. The farmer waves, and passing it brings coins. **A fourth car.** |
| 5 | **A sunset,** which comes on slowly from about a quarter of the way through the race, changing the colours rather than how bright it is. **The others use turbo** now and then, one at a time, and the child passes them back. |
| 6 | **The bubble,** a power-up the other cars bounce softly off for 8 seconds, though two cars still never go through each other. **A ramp to one side** of the road that jumps straight into a high five. |
| 7 | **A hot-air balloon** lets a gem down onto a trail of stars, worth five coins. **A slalom** of big hands through the village. |
| 8 | **A treasure chest** at the roadside, opened by reaching over to it like a big hand, worth ten coins. **Closer hands,** a power-up that brings the big hands in to 60% of the reach for 10 seconds. **A fifth car.** |
| 9 | **A light shower** over the lake, with a rainbow after it, and **the rainbow trail,** a power-up that makes each star count twice for 8 seconds. |
| 10 | **The golden lap:** the last lap's stars turn gold and each counts twice. **A giant high ten** stands at the finish line, worth ten coins to an open hand. |

Coins are counted at the top from level 2, and on the finish card with any gems, chests and the high ten. However many spill out at once, each lands at least 14 m past the last, so fewer than three are picked up a second. Missing one, or a power-up or a prize, costs nothing. Power-ups only run down while the hand is open, like turbo, so resting never wastes one, and a hand check in the middle of a race takes nothing away. A side's reach only eases by how far over the car really was, so a high five that came with closer hands doesn't make that side harder afterwards. The first time each new thing comes into sight, a small label goes up beside it for about three seconds, in the language the voice speaks, clear of the camera's picture and the prompt, and nothing new is said aloud. A label that couldn't be seen comes again later. The giant hands start fading in 260 m ahead and stand right at the finish, so the camera following the car never comes near them, and they aren't in the view of the finish. The rainbow trail is a few little rainbows a second that fade before they reach the camera, with none at all with reduced motion. At level 10 there are 19 big hands a lap instead of 13, but two on opposite sides are never less than 62 m apart, which leaves at least 70% of the time to get across. The car's speed, the laps, how far to reach, steering help and the hand never change with the level. The others still ease off at the end so the child wins, nobody uses turbo while a child rests, and while the car flies, the others make room at the edges of the road. The numbers are in `racePlan` in `rules.js`.

**Racing friends.** Up to 4 children can race each other, each on their own computer with their own camera.

- **Making a race:** a grown-up presses **Race a friend** on the title card, then **Make a race**, and tells their friend its code, 3 letters and numbers, or sends the link. A new race never gets a code that's in use: the server says so, and the game picks another. While making or joining a race, the title card shows only that, over the game dimmed and blurred, and **Let’s race!** comes under who's here. A name can be typed there too, if they'd like one, and it's remembered. **Back** goes back to the usual title card. It's the same in every game played with friends.
- **Joining:** the friend opens the link, or presses **Race a friend** and types the code, which joins as soon as it's typed. Links with the 6-letter codes from before still work.
- **Starting:** everyone presses **Let’s race!**. After the hand check, each waits on the grid until everyone is ready, and then all count down together. Whoever made the race can press **Start now** instead, and anyone can choose to race on their own.
- **The course** is the host's: their level and race length. Friends' cars take the place of the other cars, with each friend's name over theirs. A friend's car close to the child's is shown a little to one side, as the other cars keep out of the way, so it never sits in front of the camera.
- **The finish card** says who came where, and fills in as the others get there.
- **Levels and settings:** a race with friends doesn't move anyone's own level on, and every setting a grown-up chose for the hand stays as it was.

**What's sent, and where.** Nothing is sent unless a grown-up makes or joins a race. Then the game sends the name typed, who drives, the host's level and race length, and where the car is on the road 10 times a second: how far along and across, its speed, and whether it's in the air or on turbo. Nothing from the camera is ever sent. It all goes through a small server on Cloudflare ([server/](server/)), which passes it straight on to the others in the race and keeps nothing once they've gone. The code in a link comes after `#`, which browsers don't send to the web server the games come from.

**How it works.** Each race is one Durable Object on Cloudflare, named by its code, and each player keeps one WebSocket open to it. Each game drives its own car and shows the others' cars 120 ms behind what they last sent, so they move smoothly between messages. A connection that drops comes back by itself as the same player, trying again every few seconds for as long as it takes, and straight away when the wifi comes back or the laptop wakes up. Meanwhile the game stays with friends and says "Reconnecting…": pressing **Race again** then waits for the connection rather than racing alone, and being ready, or a finish, is said again once it's back. During a race the game checks the connection every 2 seconds and starts a new one if nothing has come back for 6, and between races every 10 seconds, and within 4 seconds of being ready or finishing. Someone back within two minutes, or after reloading the page, keeps their car, their race and hosting: the server keeps who has just gone even while it sleeps. A connection that has said nothing for a minute and a half has died without closing, and gives up its place to someone coming back. A code with no game behind it is told so, rather than waiting alone. The same rules run on this computer in [tools/race-server.mjs](tools/race-server.mjs) for the tests, and the other games with friends use the same server, with rooms of their own. To put the server online, run `npx wrangler login` once, then `npx wrangler deploy --config server/wrangler.jsonc`. It's served at `hemi-race.infoicy.com`.

## Mighty Moves

The coach calls one concrete move at a time, and the child's own character shows it in a looping film beside the fight, as a word or a still picture is hard to copy. Doing that move makes a big fighting move, and those are what win a round.

**The fighter is the child.** Whenever the camera can see them, the child's fighter copies them: their arms and hands, open or closed, how they lean and turn, how far they sink, their legs and their jumps. Raising the hands raises the fighter's, whatever the coach has asked for. Each big move then starts from just where the child is when the camera sees it, and carries on the way their own movement went: a squat sinks deeper and slams both palms on the floor, a jump keeps going up and over, a clap pushes out with the hands still together, and a toe touch sweeps both hands along the floor at the other fighter's feet. The child's lean and turn are copied at a little over half, as the camera is least sure of those.

Before a fight, the game checks it can see all of the child, from about 2 to 3 metres away, and says what to do if it can't. If they step out of the picture, everything waits.

**What the coach asks for, in this order** ([games/mighty-moves/moves.js](games/mighty-moves/moves.js)):

| The child | The fighter |
|---|---|
| **Claps** | **Thunder Clap** |
| **Squats** | **Earthquake Stomp** |
| **Jumps** | **Flying Smash** |
| **Rotates a hand in a big circle** | **Whirlwind** |
| **Lifts a knee** | **Knee Strike** |
| **Pushes an open hand forward** | **Palm Strike** |
| **Reaches for the sky** and holds it | **Sky Charge**, which powers up the next move |
| **Star jumps** (or steps out with arms up) | **Star Burst** |
| **Kicks** | **Power Kick** |
| **Touches toes** | **Ground Sweep** |
| **High knees**, three quick lifts of alternate legs | **Turbo Rush** |

The moves on one side say which side, and that's the one that counts: two of every three are on the side being worked on. A bar under the film fills as the body gets close to what's asked for. When a move is seen, the child is told so in words, with a chime and a green frame around their camera picture.

**Ordinary fighting moves are small hits.** A real punch, push, swing or kick that wasn't asked for still makes the fighter do it, with a small hit of 2 out of 100. There's a rest between them, and they can only ever take 25 in a round, so after that the opponent just blocks them and the coach says again what's wanted. The only way to win a round is the moves the coach asks for. The fighter stands ready between moves rather than copying the child, so nothing happens by accident.

**The computer hits hard,** at about six times a small hit or more. Every so often it's the computer's turn: it warns, the coach shows how to get out of the way, by raising the hands to block or leaning, and doing that stops the hit completely.

Saying the moves in this order makes two combos come up by themselves, a squat then a jump for **Rocket Leap**, and a sky reach then a star jump for **Power Stars**. A full power bar turns the next big move into the fighter's own finishing move.

**The open-hand rule carries over.** Hands are too small to read in a picture of a whole body, so the game takes a close-up of one hand with each frame, from the camera's full-size picture. Hands stayed readable on photos shrunk to the size they'd be at this distance, down to about 40 pixels tall even when blurred: open hands scored 0.53 or more, a fist 0.15 or less, and the other closed poses 0.31 or less. A push made with a fist doesn't count as the push: it's a small hit, and the child is asked to open their hand. A push with the hand seen open does 40% more, and a hand that can't be read gets the plain push. The summary says how many arm moves it could read and how many of those were open.

**For children with hemiplegia:**

- **A slow push counts.** Only what's asked for counts, so a careful move doesn't have to be quick to stand out.
- **What counts comes down for a child who's trying,** on squats, jumps and leg lifts, never so far that a child standing still would count.
- **Nobody gets stuck.** After 14 seconds of moving without getting a move, the coach says "Let's try another one" and doesn't ask for it again that fight.
- **How big a movement has to be** can be set, because a small movement may be a big effort. *Small* is the default, and there one arm is enough for the moves that otherwise take two.
- **Side to work on** uses the same hand choice as the other games, and moves on that side do 20% more. The summary counts each side separately.
- **Jumps can be turned off,** so the coach never asks for them when balance makes them unsafe.
- **Resting is always fine.** Against the computer, a child standing still is never hit. The coach waits, and the computer waves until they carry on. Against a friend, the friend's moves still land, as a friend's car keeps going in a race.
- **On *Gentle*, the default,** the computer gives four seconds' warning and can't knock a child out.
- **Nothing flashes.** Measured through big hits, brightness changed by less than 2% from one frame to the next. Fighters get dizzy rather than hurt.

**The fighters** are ten original characters in [core/body/roster.js](core/body/roster.js), each with their own voice and finishing move, built from simple shapes in [characters.js](core/body/characters.js). All ten share one skeleton, so any move works on any of them. To add one, add an entry to the roster with its colours and a `dress` function, and its lines to [core/body/voice/lines.json](core/body/voice/lines.json) and [the game's own](games/mighty-moves/audio/voice/lines.json). To add a film of a move, add it to [core/body/demos.js](core/body/demos.js).

**Fighting with a friend.** Two children can fight together, both of them against the computer's fighter, or against each other, each on their own computer with their own camera.

- **Making a fight:** a grown-up presses **Fight a friend** on the title card, then **Make a fight**, and tells their friend the 3-letter code, or sends the link. The friend types the code, or opens the link, and is in. A fight holds two.
- **Together or against each other:** whoever made the fight chooses, in the box where the code is or under the gear icon as **With a friend**, and the friend is shown what they chose. Together is how it comes. The friend's own choice is kept for the fights they make themselves.
- **Starting:** each chooses their own fighter, then presses **Fight!**. Against each other, each sees the other's as their opponent. After the body check, each waits until the other is ready, and then both start together, on the host's level and number of rounds.
- **Together:** the two of them fight one of the computer's fighters, the same one on both screens and neither of theirs, with the friend's fighter standing beside the child's own, copying the friend, and its name beside the child's at the top. Each child has their own coach, and the computer's turns as on their own, at the strength their own grown-up chose, but the computer can never knock either of them out, whatever that is. Its fighter has twice the health, and both children's moves take it down, on both screens: each computer sends what its child's move did, with all their moves have done that round, so one missed while the connection was down is made up by the next. The friend's fighter is seen making the friend's moves, with their names over it. A round is won by both once the computer's fighter is out, whoever's move it was, and so is the fight. Nobody is placed on the finish card. It's the level as it is on their own, with its prizes and its champion, except that no other helper comes: the friend is the helper. A friend who goes in the middle leaves the child to fight on, against a fighter with one fighter's health.
- **Against each other:** each child's coach asks them for their own moves, with their own settings. Every big move a child makes lands on their friend's fighter, on both screens, with the friend's fighter making the friend's moves and its name over it. There are no computer's turns, and every big move lands, as the child did what was asked. A round is whoever knocks the other out first.
- **Fair on both computers:** what a move does is worked out on the computer of the child who made it, and sent with it, so both screens show the same health. Each move also carries all that child's moves have done that round, so one missed while the connection was down is made up by the next. A knockout is checked with the server, so if both happen at once, the round is whoever's got there first. Gems, the shield bubble and the champion's trophy don't come in a fight with a friend, as they're about the computer's moves.
- **A friend who goes** in the middle of a fight against each other leaves it to the one still there.
- **Levels and settings:** a fight with a friend doesn't move anyone's own level on, and nobody's settings change.

**What's sent.** Only the name typed, which fighter, the host's level and number of rounds and whether together or against each other was chosen, each of the fighter's moves (which one, how much it does, and whether it's a small hit or the finishing move), and, 10 times a second, the 21 numbers the fighter copies: where the hands and feet are, how far the hips sink, how the body leans, and whether each hand is open. That's how the friend's fighter copies the friend on the other screen. Nothing from the camera is ever sent. It goes through the same server as racing friends ([server/](server/)), whose fight rooms hold two. The rules in [fight.js](games/mighty-moves/fight.js) have no screen or camera in them: against a friend, the other corner is driven by the friend's moves as they come in, through the same `perform()` the coach uses for the computer.

**Levels.** Levels go from 1 to 10, one fight each, and winning a fight moves on a level, while losing one stays. The computer's strength stays the grown-ups' choice: a level never makes it hit harder, and on Gentle it still can't knock a child out. Higher levels bring its turns a little more often, with a warning down to 80% as long, and a shorter pause between moves, and from levels 4 and 7 it has more moves. Every level from 2 also brings something new, which stays for the levels after it:

| Level | What's new |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Coins:** one for each big move that lands, and three for the fighter's own finishing move or a team move. |
| 3 | **Leaning** out of the way joins blocking, a **gem** for each time the child gets out of the way, and a **sunset**. On Medium and Tough the computer asks for leaning from level 1, so for them it isn't new. |
| 4 | **The shield bubble**, a power-up after every four big moves that land, which catches the computer's next hit that would have landed. |
| 5 | Some of the computer's turns are **two hits** in a row, one to block and one to lean from, each with its own warning, and a **medal** for each round won. |
| 6 | The computer **swings high**, to duck under with a squat, the coach's moves come in a **mixed-up order** with the two parts of a combo still together, and it's **autumn**. |
| 7 | Some moves are asked for **twice**, with a dot on the coach's card for each, and the fighter's move is stronger for it. Jumps, high knees and reaching up never are. **The super glow** makes the next two big moves stronger and fills the power bar twice as fast. |
| 8 | The computer throws **a soft ball**, caught with a clap and thrown back. |
| 9 | **A helper friend** joins in with one big move, a team move worth three coins, and it **snows**. |
| 10 | The opponent is **the champion**, crowned and a little bigger, whose turns can be any way out rather than taking turns, and beating it wins **the trophy**. |

The way out of the way a level brings is the computer's first turn in a fight at that level, and the newest power-up the first to come, so a short fight, or a quick child, still meets them. A duck or a catch missed while the child is moving is put aside for the rest of that fight, so a child who can't do it isn't hit for it again and again, and neither the high swing nor the ball hits harder than the computer's lightest move. Ducking eases as the squat that's asked for does: over the ducks in a fight, what counts comes down to just below the child's best. A ball caught at the last moment is still held a moment before it's thrown back. Power-ups last until they're used, so resting never loses one, and any left at the end of a fight fade away. Prizes are collected by themselves, and missing one costs nothing. The first time each new thing turns up in a fight, a small label names it for a moment, and nothing new is said aloud. A child standing still is never hit at any level. The numbers are in `fightLevel` in `moves.js`.

## Leap Trail

The character runs fast down the trail by itself, through a village, past fences and poles that rush by. The child's real jumps get it over what's in the way, and a jump as soon as it's asked for sends it over in a dash. Jumping is the only move asked for: there are no squats, so a child never has to work out which move is wanted, and going down before a jump is never taken for anything. All the time a child gets is set in seconds, so the running being fast doesn't ask them to move any sooner. Stars hang along the way a jump will go.

**The trail has three lanes.** The middle one is wherever the child is standing when the run starts. Stepping to one side moves the runner into the lane on that side, as in a mirror, and stepping back brings it back, with a soft swish. Obstacles fill one, two or all three lanes. One that leaves a lane free can be gone round by stepping into that lane, which counts as clearing it, or jumped; one right across needs a jump. Only the lanes it fills are painted, so the way round shows from far off, and the film and the word "Jump!" only come when the runner is in its way. The lines of stars between them are in one lane or another, and the line before a narrow obstacle leads towards its free lane. Only the lane the runner is in picks them up. A missed star costs nothing. It goes by the middle of the shoulders and hips, in torso lengths, so it's the same near the camera or far away: half a torso length out moves into a side lane, and coming back within a quarter of one returns to the middle. In between nothing changes, so standing still or swaying doesn't drift, and a move has to last 0.15 seconds, so one jumpy frame from the tracker doesn't count. A body the camera loses leaves the runner where it is. Each game uses the same ten characters, and the trail, its obstacles and the rules are all original to this game.

**A short practice comes first,** so a child knows what the game can see. Their character shows each move in a looping film, first a jump and then a step to the side, the runner copies their body beside it, and a meter shows how close they are to a jump, from standing at the bottom. As soon as a move is seen it's confirmed in words, with a chime and a green frame. If they're trying but not reaching what counts, what counts comes down to just below their best, though never as low as someone standing still. It can be skipped, and isn't repeated on the next run. There's no keyboard control: only the body moves the runner.

**Nobody fails.** A painted stretch of path before each obstacle shows where a move will count, and a move anywhere on it clears the obstacle, because the jump lasts as long as it takes. The camera adds a moment of delay to everything, so this matters. The film, a big word and the voice all say what's wanted when it comes up. If a move is missed, the obstacle is knocked aside and the run carries on, costing only the streak. A grown-up can have the runner stop and wait for the move instead, for as long as it takes.

**For children with hemiplegia:**

- **Jumping isn't the only way.** Under **How to get over things**, a grown-up can choose lifting a knee, for a child who can stand but not jump, or raising an open hand. Raising a hand can be done sitting down, as the camera then only needs the top half of the child, and then it's leaning to the side that moves across the lanes, going by the shoulders, which needs a little less. It's also the open-hand exercise: a raised fist is asked to open and doesn't count, and neither does a hand that can't be read.
- **An affected leg doesn't stop a jump counting.** It goes by the hips rising and the higher foot, so a lopsided jump is still a jump.
- **Moving across can be turned off,** under **Moving from side to side**, for a child who can't step sideways safely. The runner and every star then stay in the middle lane, and every obstacle fills the middle lane, so the whole trail is still jumps.
- **How big a movement has to be** is the same setting as in Mighty Moves. On *Small*, a bob up and down can count as a jump, and a smaller step moves across.
- **The panel says what the camera is seeing,** and what it needs right now, to help tell why a move is being missed.
- **Resting is always fine.** After 9 seconds without moving, the runner stops and waits, even when set to stumble.
- **Speed** and **length of the trail** can be set. Even *Fast* is forgiving.
- **A missed move is only a stumble,** so that the run keeps its pace. For a child who needs longer, **When a move is missed** can make the runner stop and wait for as long as it takes.
- **Nothing flashes.** Measured through jumps, landings and a long dash, at level 1 and level 10, brightness changed by less than 3% from one frame to the next. At levels 6 and 10 with every new thing on screen, through a bounce, a bubble popping and a chest, it changed by at most 4.7 out of 255, and things missed and left behind never come within 2 metres of the camera.

**Levels.** Each level is a little harder than the last, always from the pace a grown-up chose: a little faster, obstacles a little closer, and the stretch where a jump counts down to 85% of what it was. Higher levels also have more obstacles that fill one or two lanes, with the free lane switching sides, and lines of stars change lanes more often. Every level from 2 also brings something new, which stays for the levels after it:

| Level | What's new |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Coins.** Clearing an obstacle, by jumping it or by stepping round it, drops a coin, which flies to the runner by itself. |
| 3 | **Golden stars** hang over some obstacles in one lane, caught by jumping over in that lane. |
| 4 | **Fences**, and **trampolines**: in front of some obstacles one lane is a blue mat, and jumping from it bounces high, through an arch of stars. From another lane it's an ordinary jump. |
| 5 | **The magnet**, a power-up that pulls stars in from every lane for 6 seconds of running, and **lines of stars that cross a lane** halfway along. |
| 6 | **Round bales** that roll in and stop before the painted stretch, and **the bubble shield**: for 15 seconds of running, the next obstacle the runner would run into, it floats over instead, keeping the streak. A jump just as it starts to float still counts as the child's own, and the bubble is kept. |
| 7 | **Ducks:** a duck and her ducklings waddle over into their lane and settle before the painted stretch, and waddle off out of the way if the runner comes on without jumping. It's **autumn**, with a few leaves falling. |
| 8 | **Sheep**, a flock crossing the trail, and **a key** on the line of stars, which opens **a chest** of coins beside the trail a few obstacles later. |
| 9 | **A running friend**, a power-up: Giraffe, or Truffle when Giraffe is running, runs alongside for 10 seconds and catches a star for every star caught. |
| 10 | **An autumn sunset**, and **a gem** at the top of each trampoline's arch. |

What a level brings comes at least once on every trail, however short. Power-ups only run down while the runner is going, so resting, or waiting for a move, never uses them up, and a missed star, coin, power-up, key or chest costs nothing. The first time each new thing comes into view in a run, a small label at the top names it for a moment, in the language the voice speaks, and it waits while the grown-ups' panel is open; nothing new is said aloud. With **Moving from side to side** turned off there's no magnet and no lines crossing lanes, as both are about the lanes, so level 5 brings nothing new, and with the runner set to stop and wait there's no bubble shield, as it never runs into anything. The title card only says what the settings really bring. Anything left behind on the trail, such as a missed trampoline's arch, a golden star or a power-up, and the falling leaves, fades before it gets near the camera. Choosing a runner and practising are always on a summer's day. With reduced motion, no leaves fall, the ducks don't waddle, and a bounce lifts the camera no more than a jump does. Finishing a run moves on a level, as nobody loses one. The numbers are in `trailLevel` in `run.js`.

**Running with friends.** Up to 4 children can run together, each on their own computer with their own camera.

- **Making and joining:** a grown-up presses **Run with a friend** on the title card, then **Make a run**, and tells their friend the 3-letter code, or sends the link. The friend types the code, or opens the link, and is in.
- **The same trail:** everyone runs the host's trail, laid out the same on every computer from one seed the server gives at the start: their level, pace, length and lanes, with the level's obstacles, places and coins, but without power-ups, trampolines, golden stars, keys or chests, which would each need sharing out. Each child keeps their own way over things, their own move size, and whether their runner waits or stumbles.
- **Seeing each other:** each friend's runner is on the trail, as far ahead as they really are, in their lane, jumping when they jump and stumbling when they stumble, copying their child's body, with their name and face over them. One just behind is shown at the child's runner's shoulder, never between it and the camera, and one level with it in its lane a step to the side, so both are always seen.
- **The stars are shared:** a star on the trail is had by whoever gets to it first, as the server says, and it's gone from everyone else's trail. The stars for clearing an obstacle are each child's own. Everyone's stars are shown top right, and the finish card ranks them, the most first.
- **Levels and settings:** a run with friends doesn't move anyone's own level on, and nobody's settings change.

**What's sent.** Only the name typed, which runner, the host's level, length, pace and lanes, where the runner is about 10 times a second (how far along and across, how high, how fast, whether jumping or waiting, and how many stumbles), the 21 numbers it copies of the child's body, the stars caught so far, and which stars on the trail they've reached. Nothing from the camera is ever sent.

## Sky Wings

The child's two arms are the wings of a toy plane, sitting or standing, and it's real flying in all three directions. Tipping the arms banks the plane, which turns it, and raising or lowering both puts its nose up or down. The camera goes with the plane, so the whole island tips and moves as it does. The island has a smoking volcano, a town with a giant statue of Giraffe, a castle, a fair with a big wheel, farms and windmills, a harbour, lighthouses, ships at sea, hot-air balloons and thousands of trees.

**The mission:** sky pirates are hunting the child, and the child hunts them, until they've all been dropped. There are at most three in the air at once, and another comes along as soon as one is dropped. Nobody gets hurt: a plane that's dropped falls away smoking, and its pilot comes down by parachute.

**The ring** is always in the middle of the screen, and the guns fire by themselves at any plane inside it, turning green as they do. There's nothing to press and nothing to do with the hands. What's inside the ring on the screen is exactly what the guns fire at, whatever the size of the window. Marks show each plane, and an arrow at the edge of the screen says which way to turn for one that's out of sight. It turns red for one that's hunting the child.

**The limits:** there's a ceiling, nothing can fly into the land or the sea, it can only climb or bank so far, so nobody ends up upside down, and a plane heading out to sea is turned back towards the island before a wall of cloud.

**A short lesson comes first,** with the child's character showing each thing in a looping film and a pair of little wings on screen tipping along with their arms.

**For children with hemiplegia:**

- **It learns this child's arms.** However the arms are held in the lesson becomes level, so an arm that doesn't come up as far doesn't make the plane fly in circles. As far as they tip each way becomes a full turn.
- **The plane helps with lining up.** When a plane is near the ring, the child's plane turns towards it a little. How much depends on **How hard**.
- **Flying up and down can be left to the plane,** under **Flying up and down**, for a child who can't raise both arms: it follows the height of the other planes, and only turning is left to do.
- **On Gentle, the default,** the ring is big and the child can't be shot down: hearts stop at one, and they come back.
- **Resting is always fine.** With arms down the plane glides and the film asks for them back. After 4 seconds it flies itself in a gentle circle and nobody shoots at all, however long the rest goes on.
- **Nothing flashes,** and the picture's brightness is tested through firing, drops and flying right through a cloud, which fades as the plane reaches it. A shot stopped by the shield changes the brightness by under 0.1 out of 255, and flying straight through snow, no part of the picture changes by more than 1.6. There's no muzzle flash and no explosion, only small sparks and soft grey smoke.

This game doesn't read the hand at all, as the guns fire by themselves, so it doesn't practise opening the hand. It's all about the shoulders and both arms.

**Flying with friends.** Up to 4 children can fly together, each on their own computer with their own camera, the same way friends race in High Five Racers.

- **Making and joining:** under **Fly with a friend** on the title card, a grown-up presses **Make a flight** and tells their friend the 3-letter code, or sends the link. The friend types the code, or opens the link, and is in.
- **Starting:** everyone chooses a pilot and presses **Let’s fly!**. After the body check and the lesson, each waits until everyone is ready, and then all count down together, side by side. Whoever made the flight can press **Start now**, and anyone can fly on their own.
- **In the sky:** everyone flies the host's level and number of planes. Each child sees the others' planes where they really are, with a face and a name over each, and a friend's plane fires at the pirates in front of it, like the friend who helps. Each child has their own pirates, so friends help each other rather than taking each other's. A friend's character is never one of the pirates too.
- **The finish card** says how everyone did: all dropped, and how fast, or how many.
- **Levels and settings:** a flight with friends doesn't move anyone's own level on, and every setting a grown-up chose stays as it was.

Only the name typed, the pilot chosen and where the plane is, 10 times a second, go to friends, never anything from the camera.

**Levels.** Dropping every plane moves on a level, and being shot down, on Medium or Tough, stays. A level never changes the ring's size, the help with lining up, how hard the pirates shoot, Gentle's hearts or flying up and down by itself. Higher levels make the pirates a little faster and sharper in the turn, but never as fast as the child's plane, and they hunt the child a little more often and dodge for longer when hit. Being hunted gives as long to turn as ever. Every level from 2 also brings something new, which stays for the levels after it:

| Level | What's new |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Gold coins:** every pirate dropped leaves a prize, which flies to the child's plane by itself. |
| 3 | The pirates **weave** from side to side as they cross, and **gift boxes** float out in front: flying through one gives a power for a few seconds, the first being **the triple shot**, three at a time in a fan. |
| 4 | **Scouts:** small quick planes that zigzag, take fewer hits and leave a **gem**. |
| 5 | The pirates climb and dive more, **higher and lower**, and **the bubble shot** wraps a pirate up, so it floats gently down. |
| 6 | **Balloon pirates**, which drift slowly, high up, and never hunt or shoot, and a **sunset**. |
| 7 | **The snowflake shot**, which slows a pirate right down for a while, and **the shield bubble**, which the pirates' shots bounce off. |
| 8 | The last pirate is **the captain**, a bigger plane with a flag that takes more hits and leaves a medal, and pirates come in **pairs**, a leader and a wingman flying side by side. |
| 9 | **Four in the air at once**, with 6 or 9 planes to drop, **a friend who helps**, flying alongside and firing at the pirates ahead (Giraffe, or someone who isn't one of the pirates when the child flies as Giraffe), and **light snow**. |
| 10 | **The treasure airship**, just before the captain: big and slow, it takes twice the hits, never shoots and leaves a treasure chest. And **paper planes**, which find their own way to a pirate. |

The first gift box at a level holds its new power, and then they take turns. With fewer planes to drop, the kinds of pirate a level brings come first, so they still come: with three at level 8, it's a pair and the captain. The title card only says what the number of planes chosen really brings, so with three there's no "four at once". A wingman whose leader is already near comes from the distance, like any pirate, and flies over to join it. A pair stays a pair: hitting either one sends them out of the way together, and a wingman only comes hunting with its leader. A power lasts a few seconds of flying: resting doesn't use it up, and nothing fires while resting. However a flight ends, won or lost, prizes still on their way are counted, and a power, the friend and any gift box end with it. Missing a gift box costs nothing. A balloon or the airship that's dropped comes down gently with its pilot aboard; only a plane's pilot comes down by parachute. Nothing new flashes: a shot stopped by the shield is a few small sparkles that go along with the plane, and snowflakes are soft dots a few pixels across, however near the plane flies. The numbers are in `flightLevel` in `flight.js`.

## Robot Rescue

The hand is the aim, like an arcade light gun, so small movements cover the screen. An open hand fires by itself at whatever is in the crosshair, and a closed hand raises a shield and reloads. That makes one hand do two things, and a natural open and close builds up, with closing useful rather than a mistake. The tram does all the moving, from place to place, so there's no steering to do while aiming. Each place has a friend in a cage, freed by dealing with the robots there, and the last has a big robot whose weak spots open one at a time. A little film shows what to do until the child has fired and closed.

**For children with hemiplegia:**

- **Precision doesn't decide it.** Hand wobble is steadied, and the crosshair is pulled onto the nearest robot. **Reach help** sets how strongly. Even the least of it is generous.
- **Only what's read as open fires, and only what's read as closed raises the shield.** A hand that's gone, half out of the picture, too far away or overlapping the other does neither, and the crosshair shows a question mark.
- **Reloading can be turned off,** for a child who can't close their hand easily. If it's on, an empty blaster still fills itself after about 6 seconds, so nobody is stuck.
- **Resting is always fine.** With the hand gone or closed for 4 seconds, the robots stop throwing and wave, and any goo in the air goes.
- **On Gentle, the default, a child can't lose.** Hearts stop at one, and goo takes over 3 seconds to arrive. At the first stop nothing is thrown until a robot has been dropped, or 14 seconds have gone by. On the harder settings, running out of hearts ends it with "So close!", counts the robots dropped, and they can go again straight away.
- **Nothing flashes.** There's no muzzle flash and nothing lights up. The flicker check samples the picture at 576 points, so anything small and bold crossing the same points over and over counts, and a lot was calmed for it, at every level: a hit is a small drop of paint now and then, and the robot reels back a little rather than shaking; balls of paint are a little see-through, with soft edges, and the bolder kinds of fire from power-ups come less often than paint; the blaster sits back while it fires rather than kicking with every shot; robots fall apart with a short, slow tumble, the small bits shrinking away where they are, each from its own size; walkers step and bob more gently, a hopping robot's spring bounces slowly, drone rotors turn slowly, and the crosshair's marks turn slowly while it fires. The crosshair glides onto the next robot rather than jumping, keeps its firing look while it does, and doesn't bob with a hopping robot. Prizes fly up to their own count at the top rather than into the crosshair, the name of something new stays where it came up, a lid tips back while it's bonked rather than wobbling, the shield bubble from a power-up is a thin soap-bubble rim, a bubble round a trapped robot fades in and out, and goo leaves only a few drips behind it, with the dashes of its warning ring going round slowly. The shield is see-through and rises and falls at a steady rate, so a quick open and close can't flicker it. Measured with the crosshair and shield included, at level 1, at level 10, and at levels 6 and 10 with each new kind of fire among the new kinds of robot, brightness changed by less than 1% from one frame to the next, and at most about 2% of the picture flickered, all of it where robots were being hit. A haze softens the sides of the view while the tram moves, as walls going past were the one thing that flickered.

**Levels.** Each level makes walkers a little quicker and keener to move on, and peekers pop down sooner. Goo comes more often and a little faster, though never in under 2 seconds. From level 6 one more robot is out at once and one more blob of goo can be in the air, and from level 7 some robots throw a second blob straight after the first. On top of that, every level from 2 brings something new to see and play with. It's named on the title card and under the countdown, and the first time one is seen in a game, once "GO!" has gone, its name comes up over it for a moment, with nothing said. If it goes before its name can come up, the next one of its kind gets the name.

- **Level 2:** robots that fall apart leave bolts, which fly up by themselves to where they're counted at the top, and onto the finish card. Every eighth robot, from the second, leaves a power-up in a glass ball, which flies up to its own place among the power-ups at the top, shown faintly there until it arrives. The first is the triple shot: three balls at once, the other two going for whatever's near, or just the one when nothing is.
- **Level 3:** drones swoop down towards the tram and back up, and lid robots hold up a round tin lid that shots bonk off. They lower it to walk or to throw, and after eight bonks it falls off anyway.
- **Level 4:** peekers spring through the air from one cover to the next, and the bubble shot traps a robot in a floating bubble for 4 seconds, where it can't move or throw and every hit counts twice. When the bubble goes, it carries on from where it was.
- **Level 5:** robots on one big wheel roll from spot to spot and never throw. At each stop a golden robot carries the key to a treasure chest beside the cage: when it falls apart the key flies over, and that stop's chest opens with gems and stars, even if the tram is already setting off. Bigger robots leave a gem too.
- **Level 6:** a green robot with a zip down its front comes apart into two little ones, which never throw, and the shield bubble stops goo by itself, with the hand open, though it doesn't count as the child's own shield.
- **Level 7:** the boing ball, big and slow, does twice as much and bounces on to the next robot. A power-up can be a heart, but only once one has been lost: if its turn comes while every heart is there, it waits, and comes with the first power-up after one has gone.
- **Level 8:** the big one steps from side to side, and the ride goes on into a warm sunset that's just as bright. Paper planes find a robot in view by themselves, even with nothing in the crosshair, and fly round a lid. The magnet pulls a bolt out of a robot now and then as it's hit.
- **Level 9:** at each stop a drone carries one of the other friends in a little cage. Dropping it lets the cage float down under a parachute, onto the street, or onto the water beside the canal's bridge, and the friend is counted. Slow motion makes the robots and their goo go at about half speed.
- **Level 10:** the big one is the robot king, with a crown, gold on his shoulders, a medal and a cape. A piece comes off with each weak spot, and he leaves his treasure of gold stars.

Power-ups last 6 to 8 seconds, with a bar running down at the top, and wait while the child rests and between stops. The kinds of fire change what comes out of the blaster, never how it's fired: an open hand on something. Prizes and power-ups always come over by themselves, each to its own counter, so nothing can be missed. Whatever's still on its way when the tram moves on carries on over and is counted when it gets there, and whatever's still on its way at the end is counted too. On Medium or Tough, once the last heart has gone, a heart on its way doesn't bring one back. The new kinds of robot take the place of walkers and of one drone, and there's one golden robot more at each stop. There are more robots to deal with level by level, so at level 10 a game lasts about a sixth longer than at level 1, or a quarter on Fast, with about as many movements a minute. Swoops, hops, rollers and the drone with a friend are kept slow, so the crosshair, which goes with whatever it's on, doesn't dart about. The speed, Reach help, Reloading, How hard and the number of stops stay as a grown-up set them, and Gentle still can't be lost. Running out of hearts on Medium or Tough tries the same level again. The numbers are in `levelPlan` and `NEWS` in `rescue.js`.

## Tank Patrol

The camera stays still, high over one toy valley that fits on the screen, so the hand always points at the same place on the ground. That's what makes one hand enough. It uses the same hand tracking as Balloon Pop, so it starts with the hand check and can be played sitting close to the camera. Right-handed unless a grown-up picks the left.

- **A closed hand drives.** The crosshair becomes a yellow marker lying on the ground, and the tank rolls to it, finding its way round what's in the way and over the bridges, with dots showing the way.
- **An open hand fires.** The crosshair becomes a ring standing up, the turret swings to it, and the gun fires by itself at any robot tank under it, turning green. There's nothing to press. **Reach help** says how strongly the ring is pulled onto a tank near it.
- **The gun has a range,** shown by a ring on the ground. A tank out of range turns the crosshair orange and dashed, with a closed hand in it. Robot tanks keep just out of range, so closing to drive nearer and opening to fire is how it's won.

**Nobody gets hurt.** Targets are robot tanks, a few at a time, with more as others are stopped. They never explode: a stopped tank rattles to a halt in soft grey smoke, bolts and stars, its little crew climbs out and waves, and it fades. A splitting tank comes apart in soft smoke into two little ones, and the boss's cannons tip over and shrink away.

**Shells are slow and can be seen coming,** with a ring on the ground filling in where one will land, so there's time to drive away. A hit costs a heart, and hearts come back with time.

**For children with hemiplegia:**

- **Anything uncertain does nothing.** A hand that's missing, off the edge or can't be read neither fires nor drives, and it has to stay closed for a moment before the tank moves.
- **Driving** can be set to **By itself**, for a child who can't close easily: an open hand on a tank out of range drives nearer, and the tank gets out from under shells itself.
- **On Gentle, the default,** shells are slow and hearts stop at one. On Medium and Tough, running out ends it with "So close!", and they can go again straight away.
- **Resting is always fine.** With the hand gone for 3 seconds, or nothing changing for 5, the robots stop and wait, whatever was coming goes up in a puff, and nothing bad happens.
- **A little film** shows a hand closing to drive and opening to fire, until the child has done both, and comes back if nothing has happened for a while.
- **Nothing flashes.** There's no muzzle flash and no explosion, and the picture's brightness is tested through firing, stops and shells landing. At levels 6 and 10 it's also tested with every new kind of robot tank about, every new kind of fire flying, and prizes and crates rolling over, and it changed by less than 1 out of 255 from one frame to the next. Prizes, crates and names fade in and out slowly, and a bubble shrinks away rather than popping.

**Levels.** Each level makes the robot tanks a little quicker, keener to move on and keep further off, and shells come more often and a little faster, never under 80% as long. On top of that, every level from 2 brings something new to see and play with. The first time each new thing turns up in a game, once "GO!" has gone, its name floats over it for a moment, one at a time, with nothing new said, and if it goes before its name can come up, the next one of its kind gets the name. From level 2 a stopped tank drops bolts, which bounce where they fell and then roll over to the child's tank by themselves. They're counted on a chip at the top and on the finish card. From level 3 every other tank stopped leaves a crate with a power-up in it, that level's new one first. It drifts slowly over, and comes at once if the tank drives near it or the hand points at it: at level 3 it's a triple shot, three balls at once for 10 seconds, the side ones going for any other tank near the one aimed at. Scouts dash from place to place from level 3, and one more tank is about. From level 4 shield tanks come in, with a big round shield that turns slowly to face the child's tank, more slowly than the tank can drive round it. Shots at the front bounce off with a bonk and a few stars, so driving round to the side gets past it, and after four bonks the shield gets tired and folds down anyway. Tanks come in pairs from level 4. From level 5 a crate can hold bubble shots, slow see-through balls that trap a robot tank in a floating bubble for 4 seconds, where it can't move or lob and every hit counts twice, and bigger tanks drop a gem as well. Shells allow for the tank driving along from level 5. From level 6 the robots have put a friend from the other games in a cage, which the gun fires at like a tank: five hits sink its bars into the ground, and the friend jumps for joy, hops over and climbs aboard. That's counted too, and the robots let a friend go at the end anyway. Robot tanks go round the cage, as they do a house, and never stop in it. A tank that's taken half its hits runs off, once, from level 6. From level 7 digger tanks sink into the ground, go along under it as a heap of earth that can't be fired at, and come up again nearer, within reach of the gun, up to three times each, and a crate can hold a heart, but only once one has been lost: if its turn comes while every heart is there, it waits for the first crate after one has gone. Heavy tanks lob two shells at once from level 7. From level 8 the patrol goes on into a warm sunset, just as bright as the day, and a crate can hold a boing ball, a big slow ball that bounces on to two more tanks near the one it hits, or a magnet, which brings every prize and crate over at once. Two more tanks are about from level 8. From level 9 splitting tanks come apart, in soft smoke, into two little ones, and count as stopped once both are, and a crate can hold slow motion, which slows the robot tanks to just over half their speed and their shells to 60%, for 8 seconds. Bashers lob two shells too from level 9. At level 10 the last tank is the big boss, gold, with two big side cannons and a crown. It loses one cannon and then the other with each third of its hits, and its crown last, dropping bolts and a crate each time, and it leaves a treasure chest that opens into gold stars. The new kinds of tank take the place of others, so there are as many tanks as a grown-up chose. Power-ups, crates and bubbles wait while the child rests, and diggers stay where they are. Missing a prize or a crate costs nothing, and whatever's still on its way at the end is counted, while a shot still in the air when the round is over lands on nothing. On Medium or Tough, once the last heart has gone, a heart crate doesn't bring one back. Even at level 10 a game lasts at most about a sixth longer than at level 1, with at most a fifth more movements a minute, and there's still at least 80% of the time to get out from under a shell. Driving by itself still chases a tank that runs off or pops up, and gets out from under both shells. The speed, Reach help, Driving, How hard and the number of tanks stay as a grown-up set them, and Gentle still can't be lost. Running out of hearts on Medium or Tough tries the same level again. ### Playing Tank Patrol with friends

## Cannon Bay

Pirates have come for the island's treasure, and Captain Giraffe, with his long neck above the rail, asks the child to drive them off. It's the island from Sky Wings seen from the sea, from behind and a little above the ship.

**One hand does everything.** Moving it from side to side turns the ship's wheel, shown big on the screen. An open hand raises the sails, and the ship goes faster the wider it's open. A closed hand lowers them and the ship slows and drifts, which is never a failure.

**The cannons fire by themselves** at any pirate ship in a firing zone. There's one zone on each side of the ship, drawn on the water, and each glows green while its cannons fire. They only fire with the hand seen to be open. Under **Cannons**, a grown-up can have them fire with a closed hand too, as long as the hand can be seen. A hand that's missing, half out of the picture or can't be read never fires.

**The pirates** are the other nine characters, each with their own ship. There are three at a time, with another along about a second after one is beaten. They come alongside and fire slow cannonballs in high arcs, with a ring on the water showing where each will land. Being hit costs a heart, and hearts come back. Arrows at the edge of the screen say which way to steer for a ship out of sight.

**Nobody gets hurt.** Nothing explodes. A beaten ship lowers its sails and slowly sinks in soft grey smoke, its crew rows away waving, and it leaves a line of gold to sail through. Missing the gold costs nothing. A barrel raft has nobody on it, so it just comes apart, and nobody rows away from it.

**For children with hemiplegia:**

- **Steering is measured from where the hand starts,** and the ship helps keep a nearby pirate alongside. How much depends on **How hard**.
- **On Gentle, the default,** the zones are big and the ship can't be sunk: hearts stop at one. On harder settings, running out of hearts ends the battle with "So close!" and a way to go again straight away.
- **Resting is always fine.** After 4 seconds with the hand closed or gone, the pirates stop firing and sail in circles, and whatever's in the air lands harmlessly.
- **The ship can't hit anything.** It's steered gently round the coast and turned back at the edge of the world, and ships slide past each other.
- **A little film** shows the sails and the wheel until the child has done each, and comes back if they seem stuck.
- **Nothing flashes.** There are no muzzle flashes, only soft smoke and small shapes. Measured through firing, splashes and sinking, brightness changed by less than 2 out of 255 from one frame to the next.

**Levels.** Each level has pirates reload quicker and fire closer together, and sail a little faster when chasing or running. They stay alongside for less long, though never under three quarters as long, and run for it more often when hit. From level 3 a pirate running for it zigzags, from level 6 there are four pirates at once instead of three, and from level 7 each broadside has one more ball. The cannonballs fly just as slowly as ever. On top of that, every level from 2 brings something new to see and play with, named on the title card and under the countdown. The first time each is seen in a battle at the level that brings it, once "GO!" has gone, a little label with its name comes up over it for a moment, in the language the voice speaks.

- **Level 2:** beaten ships drop gems, which float over to the ship, and a power-up: the triple shot, three balls at once side by side.
- **Level 3:** a pirate tows a sailor in a cage, who floats free when it's beaten and is rescued by sailing near, and some ships drop a heart, which can take the hearts two over the usual five.
- **Level 4:** barrel rafts, with nobody on them, come apart into two little rafts that never fire, and both have to be beaten too. The shield bubble makes balls bounce off the ship.
- **Level 5:** quick little sloops, which take fewer hits, and the bubble shot, which traps a ship in a bubble for a moment, where it stops and can't fire.
- **Level 6:** a pirate carries a key, shown over its mark. It's always a plain ship or a sloop, and never the last. Picking the key up brings up a treasure chest out in front, to sail to, which opens for six gems and a power-up. The magnet brings prizes and gold over from far away.
- **Level 7:** smoke ships hide in a puff of their own smoke when hit, twice, slip away, and can't be fired at for a moment, in a light sea mist.
- **Level 8:** submarines dive and come up again beside the child, and can't be fired at under the water. The bouncing ball goes on to the next ship near enough, once.
- **Level 9:** the last captain sails a big flagship, and the snow shot slows a ship down, with pale sails, and it reloads more slowly.
- **Level 10:** the last is the pirate king's ship, whose four sails come down one at a time as it's hit, at sunset, with the light just as bright.

The new kinds of ship take turns with plain ones, the newest first, so there are as many ships as a grown-up chose. Prizes float over to the ship by themselves from near enough, and from far away with the magnet, and are picked up by the hull coming near. A treasure chest has to be sailed to unless the magnet's on, and always comes up in open water well inside the edge of the world. A prize too far away goes after about half a minute, which waits while the child rests, and missing it costs nothing. Whatever's still floating over when the battle ends carries on coming, and what's still on its way when the finish card comes up is counted there, while the rest shrinks away. A power-up lasts 10 seconds, or 12 for the magnet, with a bar running down at the top, and waits while the child rests, as do a snowball's frost and a bubble round a pirate. Once the battle is over, a heart picked up doesn't bring one back. Even at level 10 a battle lasts at most about a tenth longer than at level 1, with at most a fifth more movements a minute. The speed, the firing zones, the help with lining up, Cannons, How hard and the number of ships stay as a grown-up set them, and Gentle still can't be sunk. Being sunk on Medium or Tough tries the same level again. The numbers are in `levelPlan` and `NEWS` in `battle.js`.

## The games with a mission

These three use the same hand tracking as Balloon Pop, so they start with the hand check and can be played sitting close to the camera. Each one is a story, with the characters from the other games asking for help in their own voices, because a hard movement is easier to repeat for someone who needs it.

**Nobody waits for anyone to finish talking.** Each character has one short line, and the action doesn't stop for it: fruit flies from the first moment and all the way through the Smoothie Stand, the first ship is already calling at the Lighthouse, and the fruit is already on Giraffe's first tree. Between customers, ships and stops there's about a second or two at most.

- **Giraffe's Long Walk Home** is about closing a hand, keeping it closed while it moves, and opening it in the right place. Dropping a fruit costs nothing: it just goes back on the tree. **How far to reach** moves the tree closer to Giraffe.
- **Giraffe's Smoothie Stand** is right-handed unless a grown-up picks the left, like all of these. An open hand has to be moving to slice, so that it's a swipe. **How to slice** can make a touch enough, for slow or limited movement. A closed hand only bumps the fruit and is asked to open. Rotten fruit only comes with the last customers, and all it does is make the glove sticky for a moment. There's no way to lose.
- **The Lighthouse Keeper** is about reaching all over a big window with an open hand. Only an open hand wipes, and a closed one is asked to open. **Size of the window** sets how far there is to reach. A window only has to be mostly clean, as the last corners may be out of reach. The storm has no lightning, and the picture brightens slowly as each ship comes home.

**Levels in these three games:**

- **The Lighthouse Keeper:** the storm throws a little more at the window each level, but only while the child is wiping, so resting never brings anything new, and nothing has a time limit. Each level from 2 brings something new and keeps what came before. At level 2 a wave throws mud back onto wiped glass, and shells are hidden in the mud. At 3 a little crab walks back and forth on the glass with a pearl, and lets go of it when an open hand touches it. At 4 seaweed lands on the glass and takes a rub to come off, leaving a shell. **How it's played.** The child serves first: the ball floats up just out of reach of their hand and waits there, and after a moment drifts over to an open hand. After that, serves take turns. Every shot is worked out to go over the net, so all there is to do is get an open hand to the ball, and the computer aims right at the child's hand. - **Nothing flashes.** A hit is a small burst of short lines, a bounce a little puff of sand, and things that come and go fade or grow. ## Giraffe's Garden

Giraffe's garden has dried up in the sun, and the child waters it back to life with his watering can. It's the game about turning the hand over: with the hand held up, palm to the camera, turning it round until the back of the hand shows is turning the forearm, which with hemiplegia is the hardest movement of all, especially turning the palm up. It uses the same hand tracking as Balloon Pop, so it starts with the hand check and can be played sitting close to the camera.

**How it's played.** The open hand takes hold of the can, and the glove holds its handle. Turning the hand over tips the can, as far as the hand is turned, and once it's turned far enough the water pours from the can's rose onto whatever is below: a plant there drinks it and grows, from a dry yellow shoot to a green one with a bud, and opens its flower once it has had enough, with a sparkle and a cheer. A little short of far enough, it only trickles. The can goes on tipping as the hand goes on turning, right over, but the water always lands in the same place, just under the hand, so it doesn't wander off the plant. Giraffe looks over a bush by the fence, waves, and cheers for each flower. Every plant in flower ends the game, and the bees come to the party. The finish card has the flowers grown, how many times the hand turned over to pour, and any prizes. Nobody can lose.

**How the turn is measured** ([core/turn.js](core/turn.js)). It's measured from how the hand starts, as it's held in the hand check, so a hand that rests the other way round still has to turn to play. Its usual width there, the middle of what the check saw rather than its widest, is where turning starts from, so a hand held as it usually is reads as not turned at all. Two things in the tracker's points say how far it has gone: how wide the knuckles look for the length of the palm, which narrows as the hand turns side-on and widens again as the other side comes round, and which side of the hand faces the camera, from which way round the first and little fingers' knuckles are from the wrist, which swaps as it passes side-on. The tracker names the hand rightly from either side: on a photo of the back of a right hand it said right, 98% sure. A hand nearly side-on can look the wrong way round, as a palm squeezed to 30% of its width did, so going over past side-on only counts once it's clear and has lasted a tenth of a second. Coming back counts at once, from a much fainter look, so turning back always stops the water straight away. And the tracker's own 3D points, when they clearly face the camera the way the hand started, overrule a hand that only looks turned over. On real photos of one hand, its palm squeezed to 80% of its width read as 35°, to 60% as 50°, and the back of the hand at 45% of its width as 120°.

- **How far to turn the hand over to pour,** as a grown-up chooses: a little (35°, the default), more (50°), or right over (110°, the back of the hand to the camera). The water starts as a trickle at 85% of that, and below it there's none at all.
- **How far apart the plants are,** which is how far there is to reach: close together, medium (the default), or far apart.
- **Plants to grow:** 4, 6 (the default) or 8.
- **Resting is never punished.** Nothing in the garden has a time limit, and nothing dries out or goes away: everything is still there when the hand comes back. A closed hand, or one that can't be seen, puts the can down. After a couple of seconds it asks for the hand to open, after 15 says resting is fine, and holding the can for a few seconds without pouring, it says to turn the hand over. A turned hand keeps hold of the can even if it reads a little less open, as a turned hand can.
- **The water lands where it falls,** on what's highest under the can's rose as it is when it pours, a little wider than the plant looks, for a hand that can't aim closely, and the drops fall onto what they water.
- **Nothing flashes.** Plants grow and open slowly, water falls as small drops, a sparkle is a few small stars, and Giraffe and the creatures move gently. With reduced motion, nothing sways or bobs. Measured through pouring, growing and a flower opening, at level 1 and at level 10 with everything about, brightness changed by at most 1.2 out of 255 from one frame to the next, and nothing flickered. With a hand turning over and back several times a second, quicker than any child, 1.6% of the picture did, all of it the glove and the can copying the hand.

**Levels** ([games/giraffe-garden/garden.js](games/giraffe-garden/garden.js)). Each level needs a little more water for each flower, from 1.6 seconds of pouring at level 1 to 2.4 at level 10, so each turn is held a little longer. ### Watering Giraffe's Garden with friends

Up to 4 children can water one garden together, each on their own computer with their own camera.

- **Making and joining:** under **Play with friends** on the title card, a grown-up presses **Make a game** and tells their friends the 3-letter code, or sends the link. A friend types the code, or opens the link, and is in. A fifth is told the game already has four players.
- **Starting:** everyone presses **Water the garden**. After the hand check, each waits until everyone is ready, and then all count down together. Whoever made the game can press **Start now**, and anyone can play on their own instead.
- **One garden for everyone:** the host's number of plants, at the host's level, the plain garden without the levels' new things. Each child keeps their own hand, turn and how far apart their plants are, and each friend's can is over the same plant on every screen, however far apart each one's plants are.
- **Everyone's can:** each child sees everyone else's can, grey and see-through, with their name over it and their glove holding it, tipping as they turn and pouring as they pour, where it really is, a moment ago.
- **Watering together:** the water goes to a plant a sip at a time, a fifth of a second of pouring each. Each game claims the sips its child's can pours, and the server says whose each one is, the first to claim it having it. A plant grows by everyone's sips, so it grows the same on every screen and flowers on all of them at once, and two children watering it grow it twice as fast. A sip with no answer for a while is claimed again.
- **The end:** when the last plant flowers, everyone's game finishes together, and the finish card says how much of the water each one gave, with "You watered the most!" or "Great watering!". A game with friends doesn't move anyone's own level on, and nobody's settings change. A friend who goes takes their can with them, and everyone else waters on.

Only the name typed, the character from the other games, the host's level and number of plants, where the can is, how far it's tipped and pouring, and the sips of water each one gave go to friends. Never anything from the camera.

## Giraffe's Goal

The cup final has gone to penalties, and Giraffe's team needs a goalkeeper: the child. It's the game about opening the hand with the wrist held up. With hemiplegia the wrist drops as the fingers open, and many children can only open the hand that way, so here an open hand only counts once it's held up like a high five. It's also the game that asks for the far reaches on purpose: far out to the hand's own side, across the body and up high. It uses the same hand tracking as Balloon Pop, so it starts with the hand check and can be played sitting close to the camera.

**How it's played.** The child looks out from inside the goal: its posts down each side, its bar along the top, the pitch going away to a stand full of people, and Giraffe on the grass by one post. A kicker waits at the penalty spot until the child's hand is ready, which is open and held up, then runs up and shoots. From the moment the ball is kicked a ring shows where it will arrive, wide at first and closing as the ball comes, and the ball grows as it comes nearer. The glove is green when the hand is ready and yellow when it isn't, and the ring turns green as soon as a ready glove is at it. A ready glove on the ball saves it, from 86% of the way through its flight until 0.45 seconds after it arrives, with a slap, a ring of small stars and a cheer from Giraffe. Enough saves win the match, and the finish card has the saves and the shots, how many saves were across the body, how long the hand was ready to save, and the stars. Nobody can lose.

**How "held up" is measured** ([core/upright.js](core/upright.js)). Two things in the tracker's points say so, both from the picture. Which way the fingers point, from the wrist to the middle finger's knuckle: up counts, and so does out to the side, down to 10° below level, as a hand reaching far out points that way. Fingers hanging down don't. And how wide the knuckles look for how long the palm is: a hand tipped over towards the camera, as it is with the wrist dropped, looks shorter and just as wide. Hands held flat to the camera measured 0.66 to 0.77. How flat is flat is the hand's own, from the middle of what the hand check saw, if that's between 0.6 and 0.86, and otherwise it's the usual hand's, 0.72. A child whose wrist drops will often make the hand check that way too, and that looks wider than any flat hand, so it isn't believed, and the wrist still has to come up: held as it was in such a check, tipped 60°, a hand reads about 58°. On photos of two adult hands, the palm made 80% as tall, as it looks tipped 37°, read as 35°, at 70% as 48° (tipped 46°), and at 50% as 60° (tipped 60°). The back of the same hand and another hand read up to 8° less than they were tipped. The reading stops rising at about 62°, so every limit is well under that. The tracker's own 3D points are no use for this: for the same flat hands they read anything from 5° to 40° of tip. It's quick to say yes, after 0.09 seconds, and slower to say no, after 0.22, a hand that's held up can tip 5° more before it isn't, and a hand the tracker loses for a quarter of a second stays held up, so a wobble doesn't lose a save. In the game, a hand that was ready a moment ago still is for a quarter of a second while it's in the picture, as the tracker can also read a hand as not open for a few frames after losing it. With a hand tipping to and fro between flat and 39°, turning 12° either way, shaking, and missing from one frame in forty, it stayed ready all through a match, with *held up more* chosen.

- **How the hand has to be held to save,** as a grown-up chooses: any open hand, held up a little (tipped no more than 50°, which only asks that the wrist isn't right down, the default), held up more (40°), or right up (30°, the hand flat to the camera).
- **How far the balls go,** which is how far there is to reach: close, medium (the default) or far, which is the part of the screen they arrive in. Inside it they go, in turn, near the hand, far out to its own side, across the middle of the picture to the other side, and up high, so of every eight plain balls two are across the body, two far out and two up high, and the golden ball, the double shot and the trick shot have places of their own. The hand tracker can't see the body, so the middle of the picture stands in for the middle of the body.
- **Saves to win the match:** 6, 10 (the default) or 14.
- **The wider the hand spreads, the bigger the glove.** It grows as the thumb comes out of the palm, by up to a third, with a soft ring round it, and the first time it's big a small label says so. A thumb across the palm leaves the glove as it is, so nothing is lost by a thumb that can't come out. The glove copies the hand finger by finger, as in the other games, and a hand tipped right over towards the camera has a short glove, as the hand looks.
- **Resting is never punished.** A kicker only runs up once the hand is ready, so nothing is kicked at a child who's resting, and every shot starts with the hand open and held up. A ball that goes in costs nothing: the match is won by saves, however many shots it takes. After one goes in, the next ones take a fifth longer and come a quarter of the way nearer the glove, for each in a row up to three, until a save. After 2.5 seconds of an open hand that isn't held up it says "Hold your hand up, like a high five!" and the little film of that comes back, a closed hand is asked to open, and after 15 seconds it says resting is fine. What's asked goes along the bottom, on the grass, so it never hides a ball.
- **Nothing flashes.** A ball grows as it comes, a ring closes slowly, a save is a few small stars, and whatever comes and goes fades or slides. Measured over nine seconds of balls coming, saves and stars, at level 1 and at level 10 with everything about, and through the party once the cup is caught, brightness changed by at most 1.6 out of 255 from one frame to the next, and nothing flickered.

**Levels** ([games/giraffe-goal/goal.js](games/giraffe-goal/goal.js)). A ball takes 3.4 seconds to arrive at level 1 and 2.5 at level 10, and the wait between shots goes from 1.3 seconds to 0.8. On top of that, every level from 2 brings something new, which stays for the levels after it:

| Level | What's new |
|---|---|
| 2 | **A star for every save.** The save knocks a star out of the ball, which comes to rest a little way off and waits 7 seconds for the glove to touch it. Any hand will do. |
| 3 | **The curling shot,** from the fox: it bends out to one side on its way, and comes round to its ring. |
| 4 | **The heavy ball,** from the bear: bigger and slower, it waits at the line and has to be held back by the ready glove for 1.2 seconds in all, which needn't be all at once. Left alone for 3.5 seconds, it goes in. |
| 5 | **The double shot.** The penguin and the robot shoot one after the other, 1.5 seconds apart, the first to the hand's own side and the second across. |

| 8 | **The bubble ball,** from the baby dragon: saved, it bursts into three bubbles that drift out round it, each a star when the glove pops it. |
| 9 | **The trick shot,** from the mountain goat: a third of the way through its flight its ring hops to the other side, and the ball bends round to it. It takes a third longer, so there's as long to get across as a plain ball takes. |

Missing any of it costs nothing, stars fly up to their tally at the top, and how the hand has to be held, how far the balls go and how many saves win stay as a grown-up set them. The numbers are in `goalLevel`, `EXTRAS` and `NEWS` in `goal.js`.

## Frost Arena

A team shooter in a small snowy yard, seen through the player's own eyes: blue, with Giraffe on it, against the computer's red, and the first team to the tags wins. Unlike the rest, it's for grown-up players who know shooters, and it's meant to handle like one. It was asked for as something like the small arena of a team shooter, and it's a yard, a layout and a look of its own, with the same characters as the other games.

It's played with two hands in the air the way a shooter is played with two thumbs on the glass of a phone. **A closed hand is a thumb lifted off the glass: it does nothing. A hand that opens is a thumb put down, where it opens, and from there it's dragged,** with everything counted from that point. Closing it lifts it again, and the next time it opens is a fresh tap, wherever that is. Only a hand that's clearly open drags: one that's half closed, loosely closed, pointing or anything else stops where it is at once, as a closed one does, and stays as it was last. What each hand is doing is drawn on the screen as it is on a phone: a pad at the bottom on each side, bright while that hand is down, with a knob as far from its middle as the hand has been dragged, and faint and dashed while the hand is lifted, and a button for each thing a hand can do, with a picture of what does it, lit while it's being done. Both hands are drawn on the camera's picture too, each as the tracker has it, with a bright line between the thumb and the finger of a pinch that has been given something to do, while it's being made. The frame round that picture is green with both hands in it, and amber with one or neither.

- **The hand that isn't in play walks.** Dragged away from where it opened, the player walks that way, as fast as the hand is far out: up is forwards, down is back, and to either side is a sidestep. Four tenths of a palm's length out, about 4 cm on a grown-up's hand, is full speed, and right out forwards is a sprint, nearly half as fast again, with the rifle down across the body. Just round where it went down nothing happens, so opening a hand never sets anyone walking. A hand that goes more than twice as far as full speed takes drags that point along behind it, so stopping is never far back. **Closing it stops the walk,** and it can open again anywhere. **A thumb held up on that hand changes the magazine,** and that's a hand still down, so the walking carries on as it was.
- **The hand in play looks round.** The view turns as far as the hand is dragged across, and no further, as it follows a thumb dragged across a screen: a palm's length, about 10 cm, is 69° at the usual sensitivity, and held still it holds still. **Closing it lets go:** the view stays as it is, the closed hand can go back to wherever is comfortable, and opened there it drags on from where the view was. So turning right round is a few drags one after another, the way a thumb that has run out of glass is lifted and put down again. Only across counts. A hand moving from side to side in the air goes in an arc, a little up and down as it goes, and the yard is flat, so the view stays level and nothing a hand does tips it off a target. **A thumb held up on that hand pulls the trigger,** and that's a hand still down too, so the view goes on turning while the rifle fires. Held up, it fires as fast as it can.
- **What each hand does can be given to something else,** under the gear icon, in the same picker with picture chips that every game has for its hand shapes (see [Hand shapes](#hand-shapes)). There are five rows. **Look round** and **Walk** are what each hand drags with: an open hand, or pointing, two fingers or three, and then that shape is down and any other hand is lifted. **Fire**, **Reload** and **Sprint** can each be given to either hand, and to a thumb up, pointing, two or three fingers, a pinch of the thumb against the first, middle, ring or little finger, or the first finger curled like a trigger. A closed hand isn't one of them: that's a hand lifted. So changing the magazine can be a pinch of thumb and middle finger on the hand that looks round, say, and firing a curl of the first finger. Sprinting can be left to the walking hand dragged right out, or switched off, and changing the magazine by hand can be switched off, as an empty rifle changes its own. Firing can't be. Two things on one hand can't be given to the same shape. The buttons on the screen go to the side of the hand that does each, and the card that says what each hand does says it.
- **Shots go where the sights are, and nowhere else.** There's no aiming done for anyone. Each shot is a line from the player's eye, a little way off the middle of the sights by how steady the rifle is: steadiest standing still with the first shot, opening up with every shot after it, with walking, and most of all with sprinting, and closing again in a moment. The four ticks of the sights are as far apart as that, so they say it. Each shot kicks the view up and a little to one side, and it comes back by itself. A cross at the middle of the sights marks a hit, gold for a tag, and the sights turn red on someone who can be hit. What's in the way stops a shot, and everything in the yard is taller than a player, so what hides someone covers them.
- **Two rifles,** chosen under the gear icon. The Kalashnikov fires ten rounds a second, tags someone out with four hits, and kicks and opens up more. The M4 fires twelve and a half, needs five, and is steadier. A magazine is thirty rounds, with magazines without end: an empty rifle changes its own, which takes a little over two seconds, and it can't fire meanwhile.
- **The other team fights back.** Each of them takes a moment to take aim, and then fires a few at a time. Their shots land less often on a player who's moving, less still on one sprinting, and less from far off, so moving and keeping a distance are worth it. One that's hit from further off than it would fight from goes after whoever did it. Giraffe is worth as much as any one of them, so it's what the player does that decides a match.
- **Nobody gets hurt.** Someone hit enough is tagged out: they sit down in a few stars, and are back at their own end 3 seconds later, where nothing can hit them for a moment. Health comes back by itself after a few seconds without being hit. A mark at the edge of the sights says where a hit came from, and who tagged whom is said at the side for a few seconds.
- **Winning:** the first team to 5, 10 or 15 tags. On Medium, the default, and on Tough, every tag counts for the team that made it, the player's and Giraffe's on one side and the other team's on the other, so a match can be lost. On Easy the player can't be tagged out, the other team's tags count for nothing, and the match can't be lost.
- **With neither hand in the picture it's paused,** as with a controller put down: everyone the computer plays stops and waits, nothing lands, nothing heals, and it carries on as soon as a hand is back.

**How the hands are read** ([games/frost-arena/controls.js](games/frost-arena/controls.js)). A hand in the air trembles, and a camera's idea of where it is trembles more, and a shooter can't be played with a view that shakes or lags. So:

- **Everything is measured in the hand's own size,** in palm lengths from where each hand went down, so it's the same however far from the camera anyone sits.
- **Where each hand is goes through a filter** that holds still when the hand does and keeps up when it moves fast: the 1 € filter of Casiez, Roussel and Vogel, made to hurry when it has fallen behind, so it never creeps up on a hand that has stopped. What comes out has a hair's breadth of play in it, about a millimetre at the hand, less than anyone can move a hand on purpose, so a hand held still is a view held perfectly still. Between one look from the tracker and the next, 30 times a second, what's drawn glides to it, 60 times a second, a little ahead of a hand that's on the move.
- **Going down never moves anything.** A hand that opens is taken to be standing still just where the tracker has it at that moment, with nothing left for the filter to catch up on, however it got there while it was closed. So opening a hand never turns the view or sets anyone walking.
- **One look isn't enough.** A hand on its way from open to closed can look for a moment like one with its thumb up, and a thumb going up like a hand closing. So a hand has to be seen closed, or with its thumb up, or making whatever else something is given to, in two of the tracker's looks running before it counts: about a fifteenth of a second. Stopping doesn't wait, though: a hand that's neither open nor doing anything it's been given stops dragging at that very look, and what it has dragged stays as it was. Seen closed in two looks it's lifted, and anything else, half closed or on its way from one thing to another, after five, a sixth of a second: long enough for a thumb to go up with the hand still down where it was. A single look of something else in the middle of a drag is the tracker's slip, and the hand has gone on dragging all the while. After any longer than that the hand takes hold again where it is, and however it got there doesn't count, as it doesn't for a hand that was closed.
- **Letting go doesn't nudge the view, and pulling the trigger doesn't pull the aim.** Closing a hand, or curling its fingers under a thumb, moves the middle of it in the picture, by a tenth of a palm length or so, which would be a turn of seven degrees. So in each look of the tracker's in which the fingers have moved, the tip of some finger a sixteenth of a palm length nearer the wrist or further from it than in the last look, the hand is taken to have gone on as it was going just before, and not wherever the middle of it seems to have gone: still if it was still, and going on across for a moment if it was following someone, though never further than the middle of it went that way. What's left out like that is never more than fingers could have shifted the hand by, a quarter of a palm length for fingertips a palm length from where they were when the hand was first seen and less for less, and it's all back once the fingers are back as they were. So putting a thumb up and down, or closing a hand and opening it, any number of times, leaves the aim where it was, and a hand whose fingers the tracker can't make its mind up about, as it can't when a hand is moving fast and blurred, is never held back. A look in which the middle of the hand has gone faster than fingers shift it, two and a half palm lengths a second, is the hand itself going, and counts, one look late: unless the middle of the hand is straight back at the next look, as it is when the tracker's first look at a hand that has just changed was out. So a quick turn made as the trigger is pulled isn't swallowed, and the tracker catching up with a hand is never a turn. For an eighth of a second after the fingers of a still hand have stopped, what the tracker changes its mind by is left out as well, up to a thirtieth of a palm length.
- **What the fingers are doing is read straight from each look of the tracker,** from how far each finger is curled and how near the tip of the thumb is to the tip of each finger, rather than from the slower, steadier reading the other games use for an open hand. A hand is open by how far out its four fingers are, with the one that's least out counting as much as all four together, so a single curled finger tells: a relaxed open hand is four fifths of the way, and it's open from a half, and until it's below four tenths. The thumb doesn't come into it. A hand is closed with all four fingers curled: a fist with the thumb in against them, and a thumb up when the tip of it is clear of every fingertip by four fifths of the palm's width. Each takes a little more to stop than it took to start, so none of them flickers.
- **A hand that can't be trusted does nothing.** One that's cut off by the edge of the picture, too far away or touching the other hand never fires, holds what it had for four tenths of a second, and is lifted after that. A hand the tracker loses stops firing within about a seventh of a second, and after a third of a second it's gone, to go down afresh when it's back.

Measured in a real browser through the real tracker, three times over, with photos of hands on a fake camera, lined up so that each photo has the middle of its palm in the same place and its palm the same length, as one hand changing would (`sameHand` in [test/e2e/fake-camera.js](test/e2e/fake-camera.js)): with the hands held still the view didn't move at all in 4 seconds, and nobody walked. With them trembling a couple of millimetres either way the view moved over between a tenth and four tenths of a degree in 5 seconds. A hand that jumped a quarter of a palm length across, or four tenths, had the view half way there in 80 to 115 milliseconds and 90% of the way in 105 to 150, and it went up to 10% past before settling. A photo jumps from one place to the next in a single picture, which no hand does: in the unit tests a hand that reaches smoothly has the view 40 to 50 milliseconds behind it half way, and doesn't go past at all. A thumb put up fired 85 to 120 milliseconds later and moved the aim by under three tenths of a degree, and put down again it let go 45 to 80 milliseconds later, with the aim back within a tenth of a degree of where it had been. Closing the hand let go of the view 85 to 120 milliseconds later, without nudging it by a hundredth of a degree. Brought back closed and opened, it was down again 45 to 125 milliseconds later with the view just where it had been, and dragged again it turned 20.0 to 20.9° for a drag worth 20.6. On the other hand, a drag had the player walking 75 to 85 milliseconds later, a thumb put up changed the magazine 95 to 115 milliseconds later with the walking unchanged, and closing the hand let go of the walk about 90 milliseconds later, with the player standing still a quarter of a second after the hand closed. A real camera adds its own delay to each of those.

How far the middle of a real hand shifts in the picture as its fingers curl isn't known here, as these photos are of different hands. Taking it to be an eighth of a palm length, which would turn the view 8.6° left alone, a made-up player has the aim move by between a quarter of a degree and 1.7° as the thumb goes up on a still hand, steady or trembling, over anything from two looks of the tracker to nine. Four single shots in two seconds move it by up to 2.6° on the way, and leave it within three quarters of a degree of where it was. Closing a hand doesn't nudge the view at all, as it stops dragging at the first look in which it isn't open, and ten closings and openings leave it within three quarters of a degree. A hand that puts its thumb up while following someone, at a walking target's pace or twice that, comes out a degree off, and one that puts it up as it arrives on someone 1 to 3° off. It's at its worst in three cases. A thumb that goes up in the middle of a quick reach, with the hand at its fastest, has the whole shift count, as if nothing were done about it. A hand that stops as its thumb goes up is taken to have gone on for a moment, 4 to 6° too far. And a hand going the other way from the shift has some of its own going left out: 3° at half a palm length a second, 7° at a whole one, and the first 4° for a hand that sets off just as the thumb goes up. Whatever was left out, or taken to have happened that didn't, is put right as the thumb goes down.

**Under the gear icon:** the rifle, the sensitivity (how far the view turns as the hand is dragged: low, medium or high, which is 46°, 69° or 97° for a palm's length), the help with aiming, what each hand does and how (what each drags with, and what fires, what changes the magazine and what sprints), how hard it is, and how many tags win. The help with aiming, which is on unless it's switched off, only ever makes the view turn less for the hand while the sights are on someone or nearly, so it's easier to stay on them: it never moves the sights. Which hand looks round is the hand chosen to play with, and the two pads change sides with it.

**How fast it runs.** The yard is drawn 60 times a second, on a screen that could show more as well, which leaves the rest of the graphics card to the hand tracker. On a big, sharp screen it draws about three million pixels at most, rather than every one the screen has: a laptop's own screen at two for one has six million, twice what anyone can tell apart in a game that's moving, and drawing them all is what makes a game like this run slowly. A computer that still can't keep up, with more than half its frames late for a second and a half, gives up sharpness first and then its real shadows for soft round ones. At the foot of the panel under the gear icon, in every game played with the hands, a line says how many pictures a second this computer is drawing and how many times a second it's reading the hands, and under about 45 pictures a second, that closing other tabs and plugging the computer in can help: two copies of a game open at once halve it, and a browser saving a low battery draws fewer.

- **Nothing flashes.** There's no muzzle flash: a shot is a thin line that fades and a little soft smoke, and a hit is a puff of snow. The cross for a hit stays up while hits keep landing and fades once they stop, so it never blinks. Measured through firing, hits, a tag, a magazine change and walking, at levels 1 and 10, brightness changed by about 2 out of 255 from one frame to the next at most.

## Arrow Meadow

It's the meadow fair, and Giraffe is in the archery contest. The child holds his bow with one hand and his bowstring with the other, and enough targets hit win the contest. It's the game for both hands doing different jobs at once: one steadies while the other acts, the two reach apart, and the hand in play opens on purpose, to let go. It uses the same hand tracking as Balloon Pop, with both hands followed as in Frost Arena, so it starts with the hand check, of the hand in play, and can be played sitting close to the camera.

**How it's played.** The meadow is seen from the side, with Giraffe at one end and the targets across it. The hand in play is the string hand, and the other is the bow hand. **The string hand takes the string by not being open:** closed, or only relaxed, as a tight fist is never asked for. Pulling the two hands apart draws the bow, which bends, with the arrow coming back on the string. Raising the bow hand above the string hand tilts the bow up, and lowering it tilts it down. All the while a dotted line shows where the arrow would fly, in an arc, and what it would come to: a target it would hit has a ring round it, and anything else it would meet has a small cross on it. **Opening the string hand lets the arrow go,** along that line. It lands in the target with a thud and a few small stars, Giraffe is pleased, and another target comes somewhere new. The next arrow is on the string as soon as the hand closes again. Enough targets win the contest, and the finish card has the targets hit and the arrows it took, the most hits in a row, the arrows let go from a bow drawn right back, and the stars. Nobody can lose.

**How the two hands are read** ([games/arrow-meadow/bow.js](games/arrow-meadow/bow.js)).

- **The tilt is how much higher the bow hand is than the string hand,** 24° for each palm length, from the wrist to the middle knuckle: about 7 cm on a young child and 10 on a grown-up. Level with each other, the arrow would fly level. So it's the same wherever the two hands are in the picture, and however far from the camera anyone sits. The bow tilts from 12° down to 78° up.
- **The draw is how far apart the two hands are,** in palm lengths: from 1.6, a little more than two hands side by side, to as far as a grown-up chose for a full draw.
- **Letting go is the hand monitor's own decision that the hand is open,** as in every other game, so a fist can never do it, and a grown-up's choice of another hand shape for it, like pointing, works here too. That decision comes about a third of a second after the fingers start to open, and a hand that's opening can move. So the arrow goes where the bow was pointing 0.3 seconds before the hand was seen to be open, and not where the hands had got to by then.
- **A hand that has only just come into the picture can't take the string.** For its first moment in the picture any hand reads as not open, until the monitor has made up its mind, and that mustn't count as closing and opening. So the string hand has to have been in the picture for a third of a second, and to look closed at that very look of the tracker's, before it has the string. A hand already in the picture has the next arrow about a third of a second after it closes.
- **A hand that can't be seen does nothing.** One that's missing, cut off by the edge of the picture, too far away or touching the other one neither holds the string nor lets it go. The bow stays as it was for 0.6 seconds, in case the tracker only dropped a look, and then the string is let down, with no shot. Without the bow hand there's no bow to shoot.
- **Trembling hands hold the bow nearly still.** Where the hands are goes through the filter Frost Arena's hands do, which holds still when a hand does and keeps up when it moves.

On the screen Giraffe's bow hand goes up and down round his string hand, as the child's does, his string hand is on the string while the child's holds it, and the bow bends as far as it's drawn. With the right hand in play he stands on the right and shoots to the left, so the string hand pulls away on its own side of the picture, as in a mirror, and with the left hand the meadow is turned round. Both hands are drawn on the camera's picture.

Measured in the unit tests, with the real hand monitor on what the real model made of photos of hands: hands trembling by 3% of a palm length either way moved the tilt by under 2° in 4 seconds, a string hand that jumped most of a palm length as it opened sent its arrow within 1.5° of where it had been aimed, a hand open all along never had the string, however often it left the picture and came back, and each time a hand closed and opened was one arrow. In a real browser through the real tracker, with photos of two hands on a fake camera, shaking from frame to frame and missing from one frame in forty: held on a target for four seconds the bow moved by 1.2°, nothing was let go, and a contest of six targets took six arrows.

- **How far apart the hands have to be to draw the bow right back,** as a grown-up chooses: a little (3 palm lengths, about as far apart as a child's shoulders, the default), more (4.5) or a long way (6, both arms reaching apart). Letting go of a bow drawn less than a quarter of the way is no shot: nothing flies, and it says "Pull the string back!".
- **Help with aiming:** off, a little (the default) or a lot. With a little, an arc within 5° of one that would hit the middle of a target is pulled 60% of the way to it, all of that within 2.5° and less and less out to 5°, so the dotted line never jumps. With a lot it's 10° and 85%. The arrow flies along the line as it's shown.
- **Targets to win:** 6, 10 (the default) or 14.
- **Resting is never punished.** Nothing in the meadow has a time limit: a target waits for as long as it takes, and everything is where it was when the hands come back. An arrow that misses costs nothing, and a plain straw target always stands near, whatever else the level has brought. After three misses in a row that target grows by 15%, and again after every other miss, up to three times, and the next one is its own size again.
- **What's asked is short, and comes late.** After 3 seconds of an open string hand it says "Close your hand to hold the string!", after 3 of holding a string that isn't pulled back, "Pull the string back!", after 5 of holding a drawn bow, "Open your hand to let the arrow go!", with the little film of it, after 2.5 without the bow hand, "Hold up your other hand, for the bow!", and after 15 without an arrow it says resting is fine. Each is said out loud twice at most in a game, and only shown after that, along the bottom, on the grass, where it hides nothing.
- **A little film shows the move,** beside the title card, and then in the game until the first hit, or half a minute: one hand holding the bow still, the other, closed on the string, pulling back, and opening, and the arrow flying to the target. It comes back for a child who seems stuck.
- **Nothing flashes.** An arrow is a thin line, a hit is a few small stars, a balloon pops with a ring that fades, and whatever comes and goes grows, fades or slides. Measured over nine seconds of arrows flying, targets hit and stars, at level 1 and at level 10 with the wind, the bird and the rest about, and through the golden target and the party, brightness changed by at most 1.3 out of 255 from one frame to the next, and nothing flickered. With a hand opening and closing four times a second, quicker than any child, it changed by 0.5 at most, and nothing flickered either.

**Levels** ([games/arrow-meadow/meadow.js](games/arrow-meadow/meadow.js)). The plain target is a little smaller each level, from a tenth of the screen's height across its middle to three quarters of that, and can stand a little further off. On top of that, every level from 2 brings something new, which stays for the levels after it:

| Level | What's new |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Balloons.** One bobs up in the air on its string. An arrow pops it, for a star, and flies on. From here on, every target a level brings gives a star, counted along the top. |
| 3 | **The far target,** at the far end of the meadow, with a flag over it. A bow that's barely drawn doesn't reach it. |
| 4 | **A target on wheels,** rolling slowly to and fro on a little cart. |
| 5 | **Wind.** A flag shows which way it blows and how hard, and every arc bends with it. The dotted line bends too, so it still shows where the arrow will go. It changes with each new plain target, and never blows as hard against the arrow as with it. |
| 6 | **The hay wall,** with a low target behind it. A low arrow bonks off the bales and drops, and a high arc goes over. |
| 7 | **The bell,** up on a tall post, small and high. It rings, for two stars. |
| 8 | **The windmill,** with a target at the middle of its four sails. They turn slowly, one coming round every three seconds, and an arrow that meets one bonks off it. About a third of the time one is in the way, and the dotted line says so. Two stars. |
| 9 | **A bird with a gift,** which it carries slowly across, from the far end over Giraffe's head, every so often. An arrow in the gift is three stars. The bird itself is never hit, and a gift nobody hits costs nothing. |
| 10 | **The castle.** It stands at the far end, and once the contest is won its golden target comes up. Hitting it wins the trophy. Left for 25 seconds, it's given anyway. |

One of what the levels bring is up at a time, beside the plain target: the level's newest first, and then each in turn. The first time each comes along in a game it has a small label beside it for a couple of seconds, in the language the voice speaks, and nothing new is said out loud. Every target can be hit in any wind the level has without the bow drawn right back, which the unit tests check for each of them. How far apart the hands have to be, the help with aiming and the number of targets stay as a grown-up set them. The numbers are in `meadowLevel`, `EXTRAS` and `NEWS` in `meadow.js`.

## Giraffe's Puzzles

Giraffe's picture fell off the wall and broke into pieces, and the child puts it back together. It's the game about opening the hand and keeping it open while reaching to every part of the picture: a piece only goes with a hand that stays open, and the pieces lie on both sides of the screen, so the hand has to go out to the side, across and back. It uses the same hand tracking as Balloon Pop, so it starts with the hand check and can be played sitting close to the camera.

**How it's played.** It's Giraffe's playroom: his easel in the middle with an empty picture frame on it, the dashed outline of each piece's place in the frame, a cork board at each side with the pieces lying on it a little askew, as many on one as on the other, and Giraffe looking over the top of the easel. He says one line, "My picture fell and broke! Can you put it back together?", while the game is already going. The glove copies the hand over the table, and an open hand over a loose piece picks it up at once, with no waiting: the piece lifts a little, with a green ring round it, and goes with the hand for as long as the hand stays open. Brought near its place, it slides the last little way in, with a snap, a few small stars and a cheer from Giraffe. With every piece of a picture in, the joins fade away, it's looked at for a moment with a cheer, and the next picture comes. Every piece in place ends the game, and the finish card has the pieces put in, the pictures finished, how long a piece was carried in all, and any prizes. Nobody can lose.

**The pictures** are drawn by the game itself, with the characters, fruit and places of the other games, and no photos: Giraffe in a meadow, a fruit stall, a lighthouse, balloons up in the sky, a racing car, a picnic with friends, a sailing ship and a rocket ([games/giraffe-puzzles/pictures.js](games/giraffe-puzzles/pictures.js)). Every part of each one has something of its own in it, so a piece can be told from the others. They come in a different order each game, with the ones from the game before last.

**For children with hemiplegia:**

- **Only an open hand picks a piece up, and it fails closed.** A hand that's closed, missing, at the edge of the picture, too far away, overlapping the other one or that the tracker isn't sure of never picks anything up.
- **Nothing has to be gripped, and resting is never punished.** A hand that closes, or can't be seen, puts the piece down where it is, even over the frame, and it waits there. Nothing has a time limit and nothing goes away, and every piece stays all on the table, so none is ever out of sight. After 2.5 seconds of a closed hand it asks for it to open, and after 15 seconds without an open hand it says resting is fine.
- **A wobble doesn't drop a piece.** A hand read as closed for less than a quarter of a second keeps its piece, as the tracker can read an open hand as closed for a few frames while it moves. A piece only goes into its place while the hand really is open, so a closed hand never puts one in.
- **How many pieces** each picture is cut into, as a grown-up chooses: fewer, which are bigger and make a shorter game, the usual number (the default), or more. Each is one step of the cuts below or above what the level has.
- **How near its place** a piece has to be brought to go in: near (the default, within 0.6 of a piece's shorter side of where it goes), closer (0.4) or right on it (0.22).
- **Reach help:** none, a little (the default) or a lot, which makes the glove go 1, 1.25 or 1.6 times as far as the hand, so a smaller movement crosses the whole table. With more of it, a piece is also picked up from a little further outside it.
- **Nobody gets stuck.** A piece that has been carried for 4 seconds lights its place up in yellow. A little film under the easel, on the side away from the hand in play, shows an open hand carrying a piece to its place, until the first piece is in or for 30 seconds. If no piece has been picked up 6.5 seconds in, it says "Pick up a piece with your open hand!", and if the first piece has been carried 5 seconds, "Take it to its place in the picture!".
- **What's asked** goes in the band along the top, between the tally and the hand meter, so it's never over the table. With a long row of friends there, it goes just under the band.
- **With the left hand,** the table is turned round: the peek button is on the left and the little film on the right.
- **Nothing flashes.** A piece is a small part of the screen, a piece going in is a few small stars, pictures fade in and out rather than cutting, and whatever comes and goes fades or grows. With reduced motion, Giraffe doesn't wave, nothing bobs, and a finished picture doesn't come to life. Measured through picking up, carrying and pieces going in at level 1, brightness changed by at most 2.2 to 2.7 out of 255 from one frame to the next, and 1.5 to 2.5 through a finished picture and the next one coming. At level 10, with fewer pieces, it was 1.5 to 1.7 and 0.12 to 0.7, and nothing flickered in any of them. With a hand opening and closing more than twice a second with a piece in it, quicker than any child, it was 0.17 to 0.9, and less than 0.4% of the picture flickered.

**Levels** ([games/giraffe-puzzles/puzzle.js](games/giraffe-puzzles/puzzle.js)). The pieces get more and smaller as the levels go up, and there are fewer pictures in a game as each has more pieces, so a game is always about 12 pieces: three pictures of 4 at levels 1 and 2, two of 6 at levels 3 to 5, two of 8 at levels 6 to 8, and at levels 9 and 10 two pictures of 6 at once. A game at level 10 never has more than a third more pieces than one at level 1. With *fewer* pieces, levels 6 to 8 have twice as many as level 1, 12 against 6. On top of that, every level from 2 brings something new, which stays for the levels after it:

| Level | What's new |
|---|---|
| 2 | **A golden piece** in each picture. Putting it in its place gives a star, which flies up to the prizes. |
| 3 | **A peek button** under the frame, on the side of the hand in play. An open hand held on it for half a second, with a ring filling round it, shows the finished picture faintly in the frame for 4 seconds, as often as it's wanted. |
| 4 | **A piece the wrong way up,** on its side or upside down. It turns the right way up while it's held in an open hand, which takes up to 1.8 seconds, and only goes in once it has. Two from level 8. |
| 5 | **A piece under a leaf.** An open hand moving over the leaf brushes it away, and one held still on it does too, in 2.5 seconds. Then the piece can be picked up. |
| 6 | **Jigsaw pieces,** with knobs and holes that fit together. |
| 7 | **A piece in a bubble,** which sets off from beside Giraffe and floats slowly about the table. An open hand reaching the bubble pops it and has the piece. |

| 9 | **Two pictures at once,** side by side on the easel, with their pieces mixed up on both boards. |
| 10 | **A picture that comes to life.** A finished picture moves for the moment it's looked at, and each one gives a gem. |

Each new thing goes on a piece of its own, the newest first. The first time each comes along in a game, it has a small label beside it for a couple of seconds, in the language the voice speaks, and nothing new is said out loud. Missing any of it costs nothing, the magnet only runs down while the hand is open, prizes fly up to their tally at the top, and how many pieces, how near and reach help stay as a grown-up set them. The numbers are in `puzzleLevel`, `EXTRAS` and `NEWS` in `puzzle.js`.

## Flap, Little Chick!

A little chick has to get home to the hen house along a farm track, seen from the side, with a fox trotting along behind. It's the game about bending the wrists back, with both hands. The child's hands are the chick's wings: each time a hand comes up from hanging forwards to held up like a high five, that wing flaps and the chick rises, and while the hands stay up it glides. A flap in good time carries it over what's in the way. It was asked for as a bird or a chicken that has to fly over things or the fox will eat it, with the wrists bending back to do it, and both hands needed. Nobody is eaten: the fox never catches anyone. It uses the same hand tracking as Balloon Pop, with both hands read at once as in Frost Arena, so it starts with the hand check and can be played sitting close to the camera.

**How it's played.** The chick runs by itself, and things in the way come slowly from the right. Before each is a pale stretch of track with arrows on it, one arrow for a thing one wing gets over and two for a thing that needs both: a flap anywhere on that stretch clears the thing, even with the hands dropped again at once. One hand alone is a hop, high enough for a log or a hay bale. Both hands together, or one straight after the other, go twice as high, over a fence. The same hand again in the air only keeps the chick up, never higher than one hop: a bird needs both wings to climb, so each hand has to do its share. Logs, bales and the hay cart are safe to land on. With both hands held up the chick comes down four times as slowly, which is how a pond is crossed. Each wing on the chick is one hand, the near one the right hand's and the one behind it the left's, and lifts as far as that hand does. A gauge for each wing along the bottom lifts with it, fills as the hand comes up towards a flap, and counts that hand's flaps. The way home is 8, 12 or 16 things long, which at level 1 is a little under one, one and a half or two minutes for a child who doesn't stop. At the hen house the hens look out of the windows and Giraffe cheers. The finish card has each hand's flaps, how long each wing was held up, how often both flapped together, how many things were flown over without a stop, and the corn.

**How the wrists are read** ([games/chick-flap/wings.js](games/chick-flap/wings.js)). It's built on what [core/upright.js](core/upright.js) measures for Giraffe's Goal, from the tracker's points in the picture: which way the fingers point, and how wide the knuckles look for the length of the palm, as a hand tipped towards the camera looks shorter and just as wide. The two together are how far up a hand is: 1 held flat to the camera with its fingers up, about a half tipped right over towards the camera, nothing pointing out to the side, and less than nothing hanging down. How wide each hand looks held flat is its own, from the hand check, as in Giraffe's Goal, and a check made with the wrist dropped isn't believed. On the photos of an adult palm made shorter, it read 0.91 tipped 26°, 0.82 at 37°, 0.67 at 46°, 0.58 at 53° and 0.50 at 60°. It stops falling at about 0.47, so nothing asks for less than that.

- **A flap is a rise, not a place.** The hand has to come up by so much from the lowest it has been since its last flap, and end with its fingers pointing up. So it's each hand's own: a wrist that only comes part of the way up flaps from wherever it hangs to wherever it gets to, and no hand has to reach a line it can't. A hand that starts held up, as after the hand check, has to drop before it can flap, and each flap needs the hand to come half as far down again first.
- **How far a hand has to come up to flap,** as a grown-up chooses: a little (a rise of 0.16, the default), more (0.26) or a lot (0.36, most of the way from hanging forwards to held up). On the photos, from tipped 53° up to tipped 37° is a flap on a little and not on the others, and from tipped 46° up to flat is a flap on a little and on more.
- **The fingers don't matter.** A closed hand coming up flaps too. The fingers tend to curl as the wrist bends back, and straightening them at the same time is what Giraffe's Goal practises. A flap made with the hand open is a fifth stronger, the first one in a game has a small label saying so, and a closed hand's flap is never less than a whole one.
- **A wobble isn't a flap.** The reading is smoothed over about a fifteenth of a second, and a hand has to be seen in two looks of the tracker running before it can flap. A hand the tracker loses for less than a third of a second is where it was. One that's gone for longer counts as dropped, as low as a hand that can be seen reads, so that coming back up is a flap: a hand hanging right down is often lost.
- **A hand that can't be trusted is no wing:** one cut off by the edge of the picture, too far away, or touching the other hand, so that one hand can't lift the other.

Measured in a real browser through the real tracker, with a photo of a palm for each hand on a fake camera, held up or made half as tall, as a hand looks tipped 60° to the camera: a hand that came up was a flap 50 to 98 milliseconds later, and a wing was down again 52 to 98 milliseconds after its hand dropped. A real camera adds its own delay to each. With both hands trembling, tipping a little to and fro, turning 8° either way, shaking, and missing from one frame in forty, neither flapped in three and a half seconds held up or in three and a half hanging forwards, and played like that all the way home they made exactly the flaps the game was played with, 8 with one hand and 4 with the other, with no tumble.

**What it can't tell.** The tracker sees the hand and not the forearm, so it can't measure the wrist itself. A hand held flat to the camera has its wrist bent back by as much as the forearm leans towards the camera, so with the forearm straight up that's a straight wrist, not one bent back. A forearm swung up from the elbow with the wrist held still brings the hand up just the same, and so does an arm lifted into the picture. A hand bent back past flat looks tipped again, as the reading can't tell towards the camera from away from it. A grown-up watching will see which it is. It works as it's meant to with the forearms resting on a table and leaning towards the camera, so that it's the wrists that lift the hands.

**For children with hemiplegia:**

- **The fox can catch the chick,** as the game comes. A grown-up who played the first version, where he never could, asked for it: "make sure fox can catch, right now it just stops". A chick that stands at something has three seconds, with the fox sitting, or eight at the first thing of a flight, while the child finds out what to do, and then he's up and coming on, a little under a quarter of the chick's height nearer each second: about eleven seconds from where he starts. Each tumble, splash or bump brings him a step nearer, every time, and each thing flown over without a stop takes the chick further ahead of him. Right behind a chick on the ground, he has it: he comes up, sits, and holds it up in front of him, and that flight is over. He never takes a chick that's in the air. The card says "The fox caught the little chick!", the voice says "The fox caught the chick! Try again!", the button under it is **Try again**, and the level stays where it was. Nothing is cheered then: no fanfare, no clapping, no confetti. When he gets up and comes on, the child is told again that the fox is behind them, and nobody says that resting is fine. With no hands in the picture he waits, as the game does, so a child who has gone away isn't caught. A child who never flaps is caught at the first thing, and one who takes six seconds over each thing is caught on the way. Under **The fox** a grown-up can have him as he was, in either of the two ways below, where nothing can be lost. Flying home with friends, he's as he was too: a flock is never hunted.
- **Resting is never punished,** where the fox can't catch. A child who isn't flapping finds the chick waiting at whatever is in the way, for as long as it takes, and the fox sits down and waits too. Hands hanging forwards and hands out of the picture are both resting. After two and a half seconds it says "Lift your hands to flap!" and the little film of the move comes back, and after fifteen it says resting is fine. The scarecrow is run under with the hands down, so resting gets past him.
- **Only a flap that was too early or too small has the chick tumble** over the thing: a second's roll, with the fox a step nearer. Under **The fox** a grown-up can have it always wait for another flap, and then the fox keeps his distance whatever happens.
- **The fox who never catches anyone,** as a grown-up can choose. He starts well behind. Each tumble, splash or bump brings him a step nearer, and each thing flown over without a stop sends him back. If he gets right up behind, the mother hen flies in, carries the chick on over the next thing and sets it down further along, never back, and the fox sits down, puffing, a good way behind. Nothing is taken away, and the game can't be lost. Where he can catch, the mother hen doesn't come for that: only to lift a chick over something it has tried three times.

- **One hand can do it all.** Under **Wings**, the hand in play alone can work both wings, and the other is left alone. As it comes, each hand is a wing, so the weaker hand's share shows: on the chick, on its gauge and on the finish card. Where the fox can't catch, a child who only ever flaps one hand still gets home at every level, with tumbles at the fences, or with the hen's help when set to wait. Where he can, that child is caught at the levels with fences, which take both wings: the setting for one hand is for them.
- **There's time.** At level 1 a thing is in view for five seconds before the chick reaches it, and there are more than six seconds from one thing to the next. The time to flap is the same at every level, as it's the flight's own: 1.2 seconds for a log and 1.3 for a fence, and more with the hands kept up.
- **What's asked is short, and said twice at most:** to lift the hands, to flap both wings together once one alone has been tried at something that needs both, to keep the hands up after a splash, as the next pond comes, and to keep them down after a bump, as the next scarecrow comes.
- **Nothing flashes.** A flap is two small feathers, a landing a little dust, a splash a few drops, and the sky only changes from one level to another. Measured over nine seconds of play at level 1 and at level 10, with the tractor, the corn and the fox about, and through a tumble, a splash and the mother hen's help, brightness changed by at most 2.1 out of 255 from one frame to the next, and at most 0.2% of the picture flickered.

## Mighty Lift

The child is the strongest friend in town, and someone is stuck under something heavy: a friend under a basket, three ducklings under a tub, someone under a rock. Raising the arm lifts it off them. It's the game about raising the arm, to the shoulder and on above the head, and holding it there for a moment, with the weaker arm doing its share. It uses the whole-body tracker, but only needs the head, shoulders and arms, so it can be played sitting down.

**How it's played.** The child's character stands in a yard with a dumbbell in their hand, and its rope runs through a ring at their feet, up over a crane and down to a hook, where the heavy thing hangs over whoever is under it. Raising the arm raises the weight, live, as high as the arm goes, so it feels like lifting. Held up over the line for a moment, the friend scrambles out, says thank you in their own voice, and joins the row of faces at the top. Then the arm comes down, the weight with it, and the next friend comes along. Letting it down early only lowers the weight again: what the friend has managed is kept, nobody is hurt, and nothing has a time limit. A game is a number of lifts, as a grown-up chose, never a race against a clock, and a little gym scoreboard in the yard counts the lifts made today, for as long as the page is open. The mission is one line, "Lift it up and set them free!", said as the game starts. The finish card has the friends freed, the lifts, how long the arm was held up in all, the longest hold, and any prizes. Nobody can lose.

**A short warm-up comes first.** The character shows the move in a looping film, and the child raises the arm as high as it goes. It ends by itself once the arm has gone up and stopped rising, or 12 seconds after it first moved, for an arm that can't get far. With both arms it ends even if only one comes up. It can be skipped, and isn't repeated on the next game unless which arm lifts changes, or a grown-up has the body check done again.

**How the arm is measured** ([games/mighty-lift/lift.js](games/mighty-lift/lift.js)). The game reads how high each wrist is above its shoulder, in lengths of the child's own torso, so it's the same near the camera or far away: a hand hanging by the hip is about -0.9, level with the shoulder 0, and an arm straight up about 1.1. It goes by the middle of the last three readings, so one wild frame from the tracker doesn't count. A hand by the hip is often half hidden, so a wrist counts from 20% sure, where the shared reader asks for 50%. Each arm has its own line: as high as a grown-up chose, but never more than 90% of the way from resting to the highest that arm reached in the warm-up, and never nearer than 0.3 torso lengths to where it rests, so resting can't count. Where it rests is wherever it is as the countdown starts, unless it's held up, and wherever it stops for 2 seconds after a rescue, as a bent arm held by the chest rests higher than one that hangs. The warm-up's best is the highest the arm stayed for 0.4 seconds, as the tracker's guess at a hand by the hip can jump up for a moment: on the test footage, an arm hanging at rest read up to 0.2 torso lengths higher for seconds at a time, and up to 0.45 for a moment. A child who comes up from rest and falls short of the line twice running, or holds the arm there short of it for 4 seconds, has the line come down to 85% of the best of those tries, and the panel and the finish card say so. A try is the arm at least 0.3 torso lengths up from rest for 0.4 seconds or more, so an arm at rest never brings its line down.

- **Which arm lifts:** the arm being worked on (the default), the other one, or both together, holding a bar. With both, the weight goes as high as the lower arm, each measured against its own line, so both arms do their share, and the bar tips if one side is lower. One arm well up while the other stays down for a few seconds is asked to lift with both.
- **How high the arm has to go, at most:** a little (the hand to about the tummy), shoulder height (the default), or above the head.
- **How long to hold it up, at level 1:** short (0.6 seconds), medium (a second, the default) or long (two seconds).
- **Lifts in a game:** 5, 8 (the default) or 12. A rock or the chest counts each of its lifts.
- **How big a movement has to be,** the setting the other whole-body games share, isn't shown here, as how high is this game's own setting.
- **Resting is never punished.** After 4 seconds with the arm down it says "Lift it up!", or "Hold it up!" if the friend has started getting out, after 2.5 seconds part of the way up "A little higher!", and after 15 seconds of rest that resting is fine. An arm the camera loses stays where it was for a moment, and then comes down gently.
- **Nothing flashes.** Weights move with the arm, things come and go by growing and shrinking, and a rescue is a small burst of shapes. Measured over nine seconds of lifting, friends getting out and prizes, at level 1 and at level 10 with everything about, brightness changed by at most 1.6 out of 255 from one frame to the next, and through the last lift, the trophy and the party at most 3, and nothing flickered. ## Gobble and Grow

The child is a tiny creature that grows by eating, all the way from a bacterium to a galaxy. It's the first game played with the face: opening the mouth wide gobbles up whatever small thing is in reach, and shutting it again munches it. Opening the mouth wide and closing it again, the jaw opening and the lips closing, is what children with one weaker side of the face find hard. It's played sitting close to the camera, and needs no hands at all.

**How it's played.** Things drift slowly through the soup: half or more of them smaller than the child's creature, and the rest bigger. The creature goes where the child's face goes, like a mirror, and a small move of the head takes it right across the screen. When something small enough is in reach, a ring turns round it, and opening the mouth wide gobbles it: it goes into the creature's mouth with a gulp, and when the child's mouth shuts, the creature's shuts on it with a chomp. That's a bite, and the creature grows a little with every one, to a little over twice the size it began. Each opening of the mouth is one bite, so it has to shut before the next. A thing that's bigger than the creature can't be eaten yet: it only nudges the creature gently aside as it passes, with a label the first time, *Too big for now!*, and a few bites later the creature is big enough for it. Nothing ever eats the child's creature. The creature's own mouth opens a little as the child's does, and wide once the child's is wide enough to count, so the moment it counts can be seen. A world is won when everything there is to eat in it has been eaten: 8, 12 or 16 bites, as a grown-up chooses under **How much to eat in each world**. A little film of a mouth opening on its food and shutting shows until the first bite.

**How much the child grew.** When a world is won, the view pulls slowly back from it, over about five seconds. The world that was closes to a little bubble round the creature the child was, and the next world comes into view round it, with an everyday thing and the creature the child is now, side by side, each as big beside the others as it really would be, as far as a picture can show it: a bacterium, a grain of sand, a water flea. Words under them say *You were this big*, what the thing is, and *Now you’re a water flea!*, and above them how many times bigger that is, in round numbers: 230 times from a bacterium to a water flea, and 260 billion times from a sun to a galaxy. Past a few times bigger, the difference is drawn as less than it really is, as something hundreds of times smaller couldn't be seen at all, and the words give the real number. The same picture is on the finish card, to look at while the card is up. After the last world the view pulls back to the whole sky, full of galaxies, with every creature the child has been in a row. During play a small ladder down the left of the screen has all ten creatures, from the bacterium at the bottom to the galaxy at the top, with the one the child is now bigger than the rest, and a ring round it that fills as they eat.

**For children with hemiplegia:**

- **Only a mouth that's open wide eats, and it fails closed.** How it's told is [below](#how-the-mouth-is-read). A face that's missing, turned away, too far off or with its mouth out of the picture never eats, and the game says which: *Show me your face!*, *Look at the screen!* or *Come a little closer*.
- **The mouth sets its own line.** The check at the start asks for the mouth as wide as it will go, and after that it has to get 60% of the way from where it rests to its own widest. A mouth that opens only a little can play, and the grown-ups' panel moves the line.
- **Opening and shutting are both practised.** A bite counts when the mouth shuts, and the next one needs it to open again. A mouth that stays open on its food is asked to shut, *Close your mouth to munch!*, and after three seconds the food is swallowed anyway, so that nothing ever gets stuck: but nothing more goes in until the mouth has shut.
- **Resting never costs anything.** There's no clock, nothing is ever lost, and the things to eat keep coming for as long as it takes. Food that waits by a shut mouth for a few seconds is asked for, *Open wide to gobble it up!*, twice at most, and after 15 seconds the voice says resting is fine.
- **It can't be lost,** at any level.
- **The mouth alone.** Under **How the creature moves**, a grown-up can have the creature swim to the food by itself and wait beside it, so that opening and shutting the mouth is all there is to do. The bigger things then only nudge it a little, so that it stays by its food.
- **A small move of the head.** With steering, moving the head a tenth of the camera's picture to one side takes the creature from the middle of the screen to its edge, and less than that up and down, as a head moves less that way. **How far the head has to move** makes that a little or a lot. The creature glides after the face, so a tracker's tremble doesn't shake it, and being roughly on a thing is enough to reach it.
- **Nothing flashes.** Things drift, a bite is a few crumbs, the creature grows smoothly, and the pull back is one slow, steady move. Measured through eating and growing at level 1 and at level 10, among the stars, brightness changed by at most 1.5 out of 255 from one frame to the next, and none of the picture flickered. With a mouth opening and shutting more than twice a second, quicker than any child, it changed by at most 1.2. Through the pull back, which is the biggest change of picture there is, it changed by at most 2.9 from any of the ten worlds, even from the bright sky to the dark among the moons. To keep it that gentle, the old world's bubble uncovers the same amount of the screen each moment, the new things fade in slowly and are never much bigger than they'll be, and a frame that comes late doesn't make the picture jump to catch up. With reduced motion turned on, the picture of how much the child grew fades in instead, and nothing moves across the screen: that changed by at most 1.7.

**Levels** ([games/gobble-grow/rules.js](games/gobble-grow/rules.js) and [worlds.js](games/gobble-grow/worlds.js)). Each level is a world of its own, with a new creature to be and three new things to eat, and that's what's new at each level. Things also drift a little quicker from level to level, taking 15 seconds to cross the screen at level 1 and 11.5 at level 10, which still leaves 77% of the time to get to one, and from level 5 more of them are the bigger kinds.

| Level | The child is | In | Eating | Then, for scale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **a bacterium** | under a microscope | specks of sugar, round germs, buds of yeast | a grain of sand |
| 2 | **a water flea** | a drop of water | green algae, slipper animals, amoebas | a ladybird |
| 3 | **a fish** | a pond | water fleas, tadpoles, pond snails | a football |
| 4 | **a cat** | a garden | little fish, cheese, chicken legs | a bicycle |
| 5 | **an elephant** | the savanna | peanuts, watermelons, haystacks | a house |
| 6 | **a whale** | the ocean | shrimps, shoals of fish, giant squid | a mountain |
| 7 | **a cloud giant** | the sky | kites, hot-air balloons, rainbows | the Moon |
| 8 | **a planet** | among the moons | space rocks, comets, moons | Jupiter |
| 9 | **a sun** | among the planets | moons, small planets, planets with rings | the Sun's family of planets |
| 10 | **a galaxy** | among the stars | stars, clouds of stars, little galaxies | the whole sky |

In each world the smallest kind of thing can be eaten from the start, the middle kind after about a quarter of the bites, and the biggest after about two thirds. Each of a world's three things has a small label with its name the first time one is well in view, and so does what was too big, once it can be eaten: *Now you can eat this!*. They're in the language the voice speaks, and nothing new is said aloud. There are eight or nine things in the soup at once at every level. How much there is to eat, how wide the mouth has to open, how the creature moves and how far the head has to move stay as a grown-up set them. The numbers are in `soupLevel` in `rules.js`, and how big each creature and thing really is, which is where the times bigger come from, in `worlds.js`.

### How the mouth is read

It's in [core/face/mouth.js](core/face/mouth.js), with the rest of what a game played with the face needs beside it, for other games to use. MediaPipe's face tracker finds 478 points on the face, in a worker, as the hand tracker does, on the GPU or without one. How open the mouth is, is how far apart the insides of the lips are, measured against the distance between the outer corners of the eyes, in 3D, so that it reads the same near or far, and with the head turned or tipped. It's steadied over about a twelfth of a second.

There are no photos of faces in this folder, so what the real model measures was looked at once on old paintings, which aren't kept here (see [Not tested yet](#not-tested-yet)):

| Painting | Mouth | Lips apart, in distances between the eyes' corners |
|---|---|---|
| Mona Lisa, the Laughing Cavalier | shut | 0.01 |
| Girl with a Pearl Earring | lips a little apart | 0.07 |
| Ducreux's Self-Portrait, Yawning | a wide yawn | 0.37 to 0.46 |
| The same, with a box drawn over the mouth | covered | 0.02 |

The yawning painting was also played once as the camera in the real game, with the real model, the box drawn over its mouth for shut and taken away for open. The check found its widest at 0.38 and where it rests at 0.02, which put the line at 0.24. Uncovering and covering the mouth eight times ate the whole world, 8 bites of 8, each one when the mouth shut, with none swallowed for it. The model answered about 29 times a second, on the GPU, and on a still picture where the face was didn't move. Moved across the picture a little, the covered mouth read 0.09, still well under the line, and with the painting gone it was nobody at once.

**The mouth sets its own line.** The check before a game asks for the mouth as wide as it will go, and finds its widest opening and where it rests. It's done once the mouth has been open for a second. If the mouth hasn't opened after seven seconds it asks once more, and then the game goes on anyway. The line is 60% of the way from where the mouth rests to its widest, and the grown-ups' panel can make that anything from 30% to all of it, with the mouth shown live against its line. Where the mouth rests is the lowest it has been seen, so a mouth that was already open when the check began is put right the first time it shuts.

**Lips a little apart can never pass.** However low the settings go, the line is never below 0.12, nor less than 0.07 above where the mouth rests: lips a little apart, as for breathing or talking quietly, measured 0.07. A mouth that can't open that far can't play. To change that for a child who needs it, edit `floor` in `MOUTH_RULES`.

**It doesn't flicker.** A mouth has to be over its line for about a tenth of a second to count as open, and has to come nearly a third of the way back down from its line to where it rests before it counts as shut again: a little more to stop than to start.

**Anything uncertain counts as not open:** no face in the picture, a face turned more than about 45° to the side, or tipped that far up or down, a face too far away to trust, a mouth past the edge of the picture, or a stalled tracker. A covered mouth reads as shut, because the tracker fills in a shut mouth where it can't see one. A problem with the view shuts the mouth at once, and what was read while it lasted isn't carried over to afterwards. Of two faces in the picture, it follows the one it has been following, which to begin with is the biggest, so a grown-up looking over a shoulder doesn't play for the child.

**Where the face is,** between the eyes, is steadied too, and measured from where it was in the check, like a mirror. That's what steers.

**To add a game played with the face,** give `FaceSession` in [core/face/session.js](core/face/session.js) the game's words and settings, and an object with the same methods a hand game has: its top lists them. The camera, the mouth check, the mouth meter, the mirror with the lips drawn on it, voice, pausing and the panel all come with it, and so do levels and playing with friends, as they do for the hand games.

### Playing Gobble and Grow with friends

Up to 4 children can play, each on their own computer with their own camera: together, eating from one soup and growing as one, or against each other.

- **Making and joining:** under **Play with friends** on the title card, a grown-up presses **Make a game** and tells their friends the 3-letter code, or sends the link. A friend types the code, or opens the link, and is in. A fifth is told the game already has four players.
- **Together or against each other:** whoever made the game chooses, in the box where the code is or under the gear icon as **With friends**, and the friends are shown what they chose. Together is how it comes. A friend's own choice is kept for the games they make themselves.
- **Starting:** everyone presses **Let’s eat!**. After the mouth check, each waits until everyone is ready, and then all count down together. Whoever made the game can press **Start now**, and anyone can play on their own instead.
- **The same things for everyone:** the game has a seed, the same for everyone, and each computer works out the same things on the same paths from it, by how long it is since "GO!". Nothing about the things themselves is sent.
- **Together:** everyone is in the same soup, and there's one size, which everyone's creature is, that grows with every bite anyone takes. The first mouth to a thing has it: each game says which thing its creature gobbled, the server says whose it is, as each reaches it, and it's gone from everyone's soup. There's as much to eat as each would have alone, all added up: 24 bites for two on the host's 12. A child who rests costs nobody anything. The world is won for everyone at once, and the finish card says "You grew together!", with each one's bites, and nobody placed first or second. If a friend goes, there's as much left to eat as the rest would have.
- **Against each other:** each has their own soup, with the same things in it, and what one eats is still there for the others. The first to eat it all has grown up first, and everyone else carries on until they have too: nobody loses, and the finish card says who came where, by how long each took.
- **Friends' creatures:** each friend's creature shows, fainter, where it is in their own soup, with its mouth as far open as theirs and their name over it, and against each other how many bites they've had. They're only there to see. Nobody eats anybody.
- **The host's game:** everyone plays the host's world and how much to eat. Each child keeps their own way of moving the creature, how far the head has to move and how wide the mouth has to open.
- **The end:** with friends there's no next world to pull back to: everyone is pleased for a moment, and then comes the finish card. A game with friends doesn't move anyone's own level on.

Only the name typed, the character from the other games, whether together or against each other was chosen, where the creature is, how far its mouth is open, how many bites it has had and, together, which things it gobbled go to friends. Never anything from the camera.

## Giraffe's Paint Box

Giraffe is painting pictures for the party, and needs help colouring them in. It's the game about opening the hand and keeping it open while it moves smoothly across the picture: a reach that's held, and traced, rather than a quick touch. It uses the same hand tracking as Balloon Pop, so it starts with the hand check and can be played sitting close to the camera.

**How it's played.** A picture in outline stands on an easel, with Giraffe beside it and a row of paint pots under it. Each part of the picture has a faint dot of the paint it asks for. The open hand is the brush: the glove has a blob of its paint on the palm, and paint goes down under the middle of the hand as it moves, but only inside the part it's over, so a shaky hand still makes a tidy picture. Dipping the open hand in a pot, and holding it there for a third of a second, takes that paint, and a hand only passing along the row takes nothing. Once a part is mostly painted, 80% of it, it fills itself in, with a little sound. Every part painted, the picture comes to life for a few seconds (the balloons bob, the boat rocks, the butterfly flaps, the car's wheels spin), Giraffe cheers, and the finish card has the parts painted, the dips in the pots, and the stars and hidden friends at the levels that have them. Nobody can lose: every picture gets painted in the end.

**Keeping the picture.** **Save the picture** on the finish card, and **Save the picture as it is** in the grown-ups' panel at any time, download the sheet as a PNG file to this computer. Nothing is uploaded anywhere.

**How the paint is counted** ([games/giraffe-paint/paint.js](games/giraffe-paint/paint.js)). The sheet is 150 squares across and 100 down. Each square belongs to the part on top there, and is painted or not, and a part's share painted is counted from its squares. The screen draws the real brush strokes, cut to each part's shape, so the squares are only for counting. The pictures are drawn by the game itself from simple shapes ([pages.js](games/giraffe-paint/pages.js)): no downloaded pictures.

- **Brush size,** as a grown-up chooses: small, medium (the default) or big. Bigger is easier.
- **How much of a part to paint before it fills itself in:** about two thirds (65%), most of it (80%, the default) or nearly all (92%).
- **Reach help:** none, a little (the default) or a lot. The middle of the camera picture covers the whole easel and the pots, so a smaller movement goes further. Without it, the desk is spread over the camera picture from a quarter of the way down to nine tenths, not right to the top, so a hand painting the top of the picture still has its fingers in the picture.
- **Colours:** each part takes only the paint its dot asks for (the default), or free painting, any colour anywhere. Even free, the paint stays inside the part the brush is in.
- **The wrong paint never spoils anything.** With the wrong paint on a part, nothing lands: the part's dot grows, drops lead from it to the pot it wants, and after a moment the words say "Dip your hand in that pot!". After 6 seconds of an open hand on it with the wrong paint, the brush is given the right one, so nobody is stuck.
- **Resting is never punished.** Nothing has a time limit when playing alone and nothing goes away. A closed hand, or one that can't be seen, is the brush lifted. After 2.5 seconds it asks for the hand to open, and after 15 says resting is fine. A little film beside the picture shows an open hand dipping in a pot and painting a shape, until the first part is painted or for 25 seconds.
- **Nothing flashes.** Paint goes down a stroke at a time, a part filling in fades in, a star is printed gently, and the picture coming to life eases in and out. Measured through painting, parts filling in and the picture coming to life, at level 1 and at level 10, brightness changed by at most 1 out of 255 from one frame to the next, and nothing flickered. With a hand opening and closing four times a second, quicker than any child, 0.7% of the picture did, all of it the glove.

**Levels** ([games/giraffe-paint/paint.js](games/giraffe-paint/paint.js)). Each level is a new picture with more parts, from 9 to 18: Giraffe, balloons, a cake, a lighthouse, a garden, a butterfly, a racing car, a robot, a ship and a party. On top of that, every level from 2 brings something new:

| Level | What's new |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Rainbow paint.** A pot of rainbow paint, which changes colour along the stroke, and a part in most pictures that asks for it. |
| 3 | **The star stamp.** A pot with a star in it: with the stamp, an open hand held still for half a second prints a star, and moving on prints another. |
| 4 | **The big brush,** lying on the sheet. Touching it makes the brush 2.2 times bigger for 10 seconds of painting. It only runs down while it paints. |
| 5 | **A hidden friend.** Someone is hiding in one part, a question mark over it, and comes out once it's painted. |
| 6 | **Mirror painting,** on the butterfly: what's painted on one wing is painted on the other. |
| 7 | **The mixing dish.** Red, yellow and blue dipped into it two at a time make orange, green or purple, and a mixed paint gets a pot of its own from then on. |
| 8 | **Colour by numbers,** on the robot: each part has a number instead of a dot, and so has each pot. |
| 9 | **Paint balloons.** A balloon full of paint bursts when the hand comes to it, and splashes its paint over its part. |
| 10 | **A space to draw in.** A blank part of the party picture takes any paint, and is done once there's something drawn in it. |

The mirror and the numbers belong to their own picture. The other new things stay for the levels after them where the picture has room. The first time each new thing comes along in a game, it has a small label beside it for a couple of seconds, in the language the voice speaks, and nothing new is said out loud. Missing any of it costs nothing, and the brush size, how much is enough, reach help and the colour rule stay as a grown-up set them. A level takes a pretend child about 34 seconds at level 1 and about 44 at the longest. The numbers are in `paintLevel`, `EXTRAS` and `NEWS` in `paint.js`.

### Painting with friends

Up to 4 children can paint, each on their own computer with their own camera, together (the default) or against each other, as whoever makes the game chooses under **Play with friends**.

- **Making and joining:** a grown-up presses **Make a game**, chooses together or against each other, and tells their friends the 3-letter code, or sends the link. A friend types the code, or opens the link, and is in. Everyone presses **Start painting**, and after the hand check they count down together.
- **The picture:** the host's level's picture, plain, without the levels' new things, by the host's colour rule and how much is enough. Each child keeps their own brush size and reach help. A game with friends doesn't move anyone's own level on.
- **Together:** everyone paints the same sheet. Each child sees everyone else's glove, grey and see-through, with their name, and their strokes arrive on the sheet a moment after they're painted, so every sheet ends up the same. Each part belongs to whoever's stroke finished it: the game claims it, and the server says whose it is, the first to claim it having it. Two children painting the same part at once can each have it finished on their sheet by the other's strokes as they arrive, so a part that's done and still nobody's after half a second is claimed by everyone who painted some of it, and goes to the first. The finish card says how many parts each one finished, and nobody is placed.
- **Against each other:** each paints their own copy, and sees how far the others have got, as a percent, at the top. The first to finish is first. After 3 minutes everyone's picture is done, as far as it got, and the rest are placed by how much they painted.
- **The end:** a friend who goes takes their glove with them, what they painted stays painted, and everyone else paints on.

Only the name typed, the character from the other games, whether together or against each other was chosen, the host's level, colour rule and how much is enough, where the brush is on the sheet, whether it's down, its paint and size, the glove's shape, the strokes painted as short lists of numbers, which parts are done, and how far along each picture is go to friends. Never anything from the camera. The server passes on only strokes that make sense ([server/race-room.js](server/race-room.js), `cleanBrush`).

## Moto Mountain

The child races a motorbike up a winding mountain road, against other riders, over steep hills that throw the bike into the air, all the way to the top. The bike rides by itself, and the child leans their body to the side, and the bike leans the same way. In a bend the bike drifts to the outside, and leaning into the bend holds it on the road: a gentle bend needs a small lean, a tight one a deeper lean, held until the road straightens. It's the game about leaning the trunk to each side and holding it there, shifting the weight sideways, which children with hemiplegia find hard, above all towards the weaker side. It uses the whole-body tracker, but only needs the head and shoulders, so it can be played sitting on a chair or a stool that won't tip, or standing. The camera needs room to lean to each side, and reads the lean best with the hips in the picture too.

**How it's played.** The bike is seen from behind, going up the road. Along the bottom of the screen is a bar with a dot, which is the child's lean, and a green stretch, which is where the road needs the lean to be. An arrow over it shows which way the bend that's coming goes, from 60 metres before it, and is bigger for the big bend. Boards with arrows stand round the outside of every bend. The child's own picture is bottom left. Until the first lean of a ride, and whenever the bike is on the grass on the outside of a bend, the character shows the lean in a looping film, with "Lean to the left!" or "Lean to the right!" under it and a bar that fills as the lean gets near what the bend needs. The mission is one line, "Lean into the bends, and ride to the top!", said as the ride starts. Along the top are the bends ridden, how many of them were ridden all on the road, and any prizes. The finish card has the bends ridden all on the road, how many times the child leaned to each side, the longest lean held, and any prizes. Nobody can lose, and every ride that's finished moves on a level.

**The wild ride** is how the game comes ([games/moto-mountain/ride.js](games/moto-mountain/ride.js), `WILD`, `RIVAL` and `rise`). A grown-up who tried the first version, which was the road alone, asked for it to be much more of a challenge: other riders to race, things in the way, hills up and down, and more of a jumping game than a road. The road alone is still there, as **Gentle**, under the gear icon.

- **A race.** Four other riders, five from level 6, line up in front of the child, who starts last. Each rides at their own speed, from as fast as the child's bike to 16% faster, leans into the bends, goes round what's in the way, takes the ramps and flies off the hills. Each of them fails to get out of the way of about one row of hay bales in seven, goes through it and is slowed for 2 seconds, which is where they're caught. The race is kept close: a rider 70 metres in front has eased off by 14%, and one 70 metres behind is trying 14% harder. With nobody in the picture, they stop and wait too. Where the child is in the race is at the top left, and a place gained is said big over the bike. The finish card says where they came. First, second or third moves the game on a level. Fourth or worse, and it's "Let's try level 3 again!", with **Try again** as the button.
- **Hills.** Most straights have a steep hill, 9.5 to 11.5 metres up in 30, about 20 degrees. A bike going at more than 6 metres a second leaves the ground at the top, where the road falls away from under it faster than it can fall, and comes down further on: about 1.7 seconds and 25 metres at the usual speed, and further for a faster bike. A bike is slower up a hill and faster down one. One bend in three goes down hill, by 3 to 5.5 metres, and one in three up.
- **Ramps and hay bales.** A row of hay bales across half the road, to one side or down the middle, has to be steered round. Bales right across the road, grass and all, have a ramp 13 metres before them at one side, and only a bike off the ramp gets over. Going through bales bursts them, in a puff of straw, and leaves the bike 30% of its speed. They're soft, nobody falls off, and the bike gets going again by itself.
- **Tricks.** In the air a lean doesn't steer the bike, which keeps its line. So a lean held there, 60% of a whole lean for half a second, is a trick: the bike tips over with it, and lands with a burst of speed, 30% faster for 1.6 seconds. "Lean in the air for a trick!" is asked for on the first four jumps, until one is landed. It's the game's own move with nothing in the way of it, and it's what wins a race: the child leans deep and is upright again in time to land.
- **Stars in the air,** past the top of each hill and each ramp, are only for a bike that's flying.
- **A river to jump,** from level 7, in place of the gentle ride's jump, as the wild ride has jumps from the start. Its near bank is a ramp right across the road, 3 metres high, and the water is 11 metres of the road. A bike going well flies it. One that's slow comes down in it and wades, at 40% of its speed, and comes out the other side.
- **More of it as the levels go up.** At level 1, four straights in five have a hill, bales or a ramp, and nearly all of them at level 10. A second jump follows the first in 15% of the straights at level 1 and 70% at level 10, so a ride of ten bends has about 7 jumps at level 1 and about 11 at level 10, and takes about 105 seconds at level 1 and about 125 at level 10.
- **What's still kind.** Nothing is lost but the race. The grass still only slows the bike, a bike with no lean still waits in its bend and is helped round after 10 seconds, and a child who never leans at all still gets to the top, last. A game with friends is the road alone, with its stars and speed arrows: friends are each other's race.
- **How hard it is,** with pretend children on six roads at level 1, against the riders as they come: one who leans as the road needs and steers round the bales, with no tricks, came second or third of five; the same with a trick in every jump came first or second; and one whose lean is 60% of what the bends need, and slow to come, came third or fourth. Against the riders a grown-up can choose as hard to beat, the first of those came third, and against the easy ones first. No real child or grown-up has ridden it.

**A short warm-up comes first,** before the first ride. The character shows the move, and the child leans as far as they can to one side and holds it, and then to the other. The side being worked on comes second, once the child knows what's wanted. A lean counts as far as it was held for 0.8 seconds, so a lurch isn't taken for it, and a side is over once the child comes back to under 45% of it, or after 9 seconds. A whole lean to each side is then 85% of what the child held there, but never more than the grown-ups' setting, and never less than 3.5 degrees. Less than 4 degrees isn't taken as a measured lean. A side that wasn't measured, and both sides when the warm-up is skipped with **Skip the warm-up**, ask for a whole lean of 8 degrees, or the grown-ups' setting if that's smaller: nothing is known of how far that child leans, so little is asked. The warm-up isn't repeated on the next ride unless a grown-up has the body check done again. Wherever the child sits through the countdown is taken as upright, unless they're leaning more than 10 degrees, so a child who sits a little crooked rides straight.

**How the lean is read** ([games/moto-mountain/ride.js](games/moto-mountain/ride.js), `leanOf` and `Leaner`). Only the lean is read: the tilt of the line from the middle of the hips to the middle of the shoulders, averaged with the tilt of the line between the shoulders. The trunk is what shifting the weight sideways moves, and a shoulder alone can be hitched up without leaning at all, so both are read. A body leaning all in a piece tips both the same, and is read as far as it leans: a filmed person tipped over by 8 and by 16 degrees was read as 8.6 and 16.2. A body bending at the waist is read as a little under half of the bend at the top of its spine: on footage of a person bending evenly from 20 degrees one way to 20 the other, put through the real tracker, 0.43 of a degree was read for each degree of bend, never more than 1.2 degrees off a straight line, and the same each way. So the degrees in the settings are degrees as the camera reads them, and the warm-up, which measures the child the same way, is what makes a whole lean fit them. With the hips hidden, as behind a table, it's the shoulders alone, which read a little more (0.56 of a degree for each degree of bend). Hips that come into the picture or go out of it, as at its bottom edge, change the reading little by little, over about a second, so the bike is never jolted by them. Shoulders one behind the other, as when the child turns away, say nothing. The lean is measured against the child's own whole lean to that side, and near upright nothing counts: 14% of a whole lean, and never less than 1.6 degrees, which is what the tracker wobbles by. One jump of more than 22 degrees between two looks is taken as the tracker's mistake, unless it's still there two looks later. With nobody to see, the bike comes upright by itself, gently.

**How a lean turns the bike** ([games/moto-mountain/ride.js](games/moto-mountain/ride.js), `turnOf` and `STEER`). The bike doesn't jump across the road when the child leans. A lean swings its nose round, little by little, and the bike goes where its nose points: a bend swings it towards the bend's outside by as much as the lean it needs would swing it back, and the road draws it into line again and towards its middle. The more the child leans, the more the bike turns, by a logarithm: half a whole lean turns it 0.63 as much as a whole one, two whole leans 1.46 times as much, and three, which is as far as counts, 1.77 times. So the first of a lean does the most, leaning further always does more, by less and less, and there's no lean at which the bike suddenly does a lot or stops doing more. The bike tips over the same way: about 14 degrees in an ordinary bend, 21 for a whole lean and 38 at the very most, and it takes about half a second to get there. The dot on the bar goes by the turn too, so three whole leans take it to the end of the bar.

This is the second way it has worked. In the first, the bike went sideways at once, as fast as the lean was big, tipped a whole 32 degrees for one whole lean, and did no more for leaning further than a little over one. A grown-up who tried it with their own body said the movement was too drastic, that it should be slower, and that leaning more should turn the bike more, by a logarithm. With no lean at all in a bend that needs a little over half a whole lean, the first one had the bike off the road 0.9 seconds after the bend had come on, and this one after 1.9, with its nose swung 10 degrees round. Leaning as little as 0.45 or as much as 1.5 times what a bend needs keeps the bike on the road at level 1, and from 0.6 to 1.5 times at level 10. The new way has only been ridden by pretend children and by the filmed people in the tests.

**The kind rules.**

- **Resting on a straight costs nothing.** The road draws the bike back to the middle by itself.
- **The grass only slows the bike.** Beside the road is 2.4 metres of grass, and the bike keeps 45% of its speed on it. Nothing is lost, and the bend just isn't counted as ridden all on the road. Each bend ridden all on the road makes the bike 4% faster, up to four in a row, until it next touches the grass.
- **A bike on the grass on the outside of a bend waits there.** With no lean into the bend for 1.2 seconds, it stops, and the smallest lean into the bend gets it going again. After 5 seconds stopped, the child is told "Take your time. I'll wait for you." After 10 seconds stopped, with the child in the picture, the bike is helped round that bend slowly, so nobody is ever stuck and a ride can't be lost. The finish card says how many bends it was helped round.
- **The bends come down to meet a child who's trying.** Leaning the right way but not far enough, on the grass on the outside of a bend, for 3 seconds has a whole lean to that side come down by 15%, and the finish card says so.
- **With nobody in the picture,** for 0.6 seconds, the bike stops and waits, and the child is asked to come back.
- **Nothing flashes.** The camera tips with the bike only a little, 22% of the bike's lean, and not at all with reduced motion turned on in the computer's settings. At the finish the camera stays behind the bike and moves over to one side: it never swings round, which would sweep the whole picture past in a moment. The banners over the start and the finish are about as bright as the sky behind them, with no ink line round them, and the finish's squares are grey and cream, not black and white. The bike rides under them, and the first ones, which were darker, filled the top of the picture for a moment and changed its brightness by up to 5.5 out of 255 in one frame, where the most the games allow is 4. Measured in the browser tests now, the biggest change of the whole picture between two frames was 1.7 setting off under the start banner, between 0.9 and 2.1 riding the bends at levels 1 and 10 (it varies from run to run), up to 1.0 on the grass with the dust coming up, and 2.4 through the snow, under the finish banner and up to the top, with nothing flickering in any of them. On the wild ride, three things are done for it. Sparks made while the bike is in the air go along with the bike, and don't hang where they were made: the camera is a few metres behind the bike and would fly through them half a second later, and a burst of pale stars filled the middle of the picture there for one frame and changed its brightness by 5.8. The hay bales and the ramps are about as bright as the road and the grass they lie on, as a row of bales right across the road goes by close under a bike that's flying over it. And in the air the camera looks further up the road, and takes its height from a stretch of road and not from one place, so that a river's bank, three metres up and down again in a few, doesn't tip the whole picture over its top. Measured in the browser tests, the biggest change between two frames was between 1.1 and 2.1 setting off among the other riders, over a hill and on the road at levels 1 and 10, 2.2 going through a row of bales, 2.6 flying over one off its ramp, 2.7 flying a river and 0.9 wading one, with other tests running on the same computer at the time, which makes it worse.

**The grown-ups' settings,** under the gear icon:

- **The ride:** wild (the default), which is the race over the hills, or gentle, which is the road alone, with nobody to race and nothing in the way.
- **The other riders in the race:** easy to beat (93% of their own speed), a fair race (103.5%, the default) or hard to beat (109%).
- **How far a whole lean is, at most:** a little (about 7 degrees from upright), some (10), medium (14, the default) or a lot (19). It's never more than 85% of the child's own lean to that side from the warm-up.
- **Which way the bends go:** one way and then the other (the default), or two in every three towards the side being worked on. Then the bends that way are the shorter ones, held for 1.4 to 2.2 seconds, and the others the longer, 3.2 to 4.2, so the road doesn't wind round and round. Otherwise a bend is held for 1.4 to 3.4 seconds at the usual speed. The side being worked on is chosen in the same panel, and is the same in every game.
- **Bends in a ride:** 6, 10 (the default) or 14.
- **How fast the bike goes,** which is how long there is to lean into a bend: gently (78% of the usual speed), the usual speed, or quickly (118%).
- **How much help the road gives:** how far the bike's turn can be from what the bend needs, with the bike still on the road in the end. Lots (42% of a whole lean's turn, so leaning the right way is nearly enough), some (30%, the default) or a little (20%). With more help the bike's nose swings round more slowly for the same lean.
- **With friends:** together or against each other.

**Levels** ([games/moto-mountain/ride.js](games/moto-mountain/ride.js), `rideLevel` and `NEW_THINGS`). Level 1 is the plain road. A bend asks for a little over half a whole lean at level 1, from 50% to 58%, and up to about three quarters at level 10, from 60% to 78%. The bike is a fifth faster by level 10, 18.2 metres a second instead of 15. A tighter bend comes on over a longer stretch, so there's as long to lean into it as a gentle one at level 1. The mountain changes as the levels go up: a meadow at levels 1 to 3, pine woods at 4 to 6, bare rock at 7 to 9, and snow on the top at 10. On top of that, every level from 2 brings something new, which stays for the levels after it:

| Level | What's new |

| 2 | **Gold stars:** three down the middle of every bend, for the bike leaning as far as the bend needs, and now and then a row of three to one side of a straight. |
| 3 | **Puddles** to one side of a straight: a splash, and slower for a moment. |
| 4 | **Speed arrows** to one side of a straight: faster for two seconds. |

- **Making and joining:** under **Play with friends** on the title card, a grown-up presses **Make a ride** and tells their friends the 3-letter code, or sends the link. A friend types the code, or opens the link, and is in. A fifth is told the ride already has four players.
- **Together or against each other:** whoever made the ride chooses, in the box where the code is or under the gear icon as **With friends**, and the others are shown what they chose. Together is how it comes.
- **Starting:** everyone chooses a rider and presses **Let's ride!**. After the body check and the warm-up, each waits until everyone is ready, and then all count down together.
- **The same road for everyone:** the host's level, number of bends and speed, laid out the same on every computer, with the bends one way and then the other. It's the plain road with its stars and speed arrows, and none of the levels' other new things. Each child keeps their own lean and their own help. Each friend's bike is on the road with their face and name over it.
- **Together** (the default): one convoy. Whoever is more than 45 metres in front of the last rider eases off, down to half speed, and whoever is more than 25 metres behind the first gets a tow, a fifth faster. Nobody is stopped by it. The stars everyone catches are added up, and shown top right with each one's own, and the finish card says "You rode to the top together!".
- **Against each other:** a race to the top, placed by time. Who's in front is shown top right, and everyone rides on to the finish. The first there is "First to the top!".

## Rooftop Rope

Ropes are strung between rooftops, high over a city, and the child's walker crosses them while the child stands on one leg. With one foot held off the floor, the walker rolls along the rope on its one wheel, standing on it on one foot, faster the higher the foot is held. With both feet down on a rope, the walker stands still and wobbles more and more, and in the end drops slowly into a net under the rope, bounces, and starts that rope again from its rooftop. It's the game about standing on one leg and holding it, and lifting the foot high, on each side: balance, which children with hemiplegia find hard, above all standing on the weaker leg. It uses the whole-body tracker and is played standing, with all of the child in the picture, feet too. A hand on a steady chair, a wall or a grown-up is fine: it's the foot off the floor that counts. The title card and the grown-ups' panel say to clear some space, keep something steady within reach and stay close by.

**How it's played.** The walker is seen from behind, so the child's left is the walker's left. On a rooftop it stands on its own two feet. On a rope it's up on a wheel, on a plate over a fat tyre, which comes out from under it as it steps onto the rope and is put away as it steps off: the wheel turns as it goes, the pale blocks round its tread showing how fast, and rocks to and fro under the walker when it wobbles. A grown-up who saw the first version, where the walker slid along the rope on a bare foot, asked for some kind of wheel for one foot where the child has to stand with a foot up. Friends' walkers each ride a wheel of their own colour. The child's own picture is bottom left, with a tall meter beside it: the dot is the lifted foot, the line low down is where the walker starts to go, and the green at the top is a whole lift. Two footprints at the bottom of the screen show the foot to lift, in yellow, and the one that's up, in green and raised. A bar at the top fills as the walker wobbles, and the rope creaks louder with it. While the walker waits for a foot to go up, on a rooftop or stopped on a rope, the character shows the lift in a looping film, with "Lift your left foot!" or "Lift your right foot!" under it, or "Lift one foot!" when the child lifts whichever they like, and the other foot up on a rope that's for this one is "Now the other foot!". Off the end of a rope, the walker walks across the rooftop by itself, in a second and a half, to where the next rope starts, so the child can put the foot down as soon as the rope is crossed. The mission is one line, "Stand on one leg, and cross to the last rooftop!", said as the game starts. Along the top are the ropes crossed and any prizes. The finish card has the ropes crossed, how long the child stood on each leg, the longest stand, how many times the walker dropped into the net, and any prizes. Nobody can lose, and every game that's finished moves on a level.

**How fast the walker goes.** A foot 40% of the way to a whole lift is off the floor, and the walker rolls at half its speed. The higher the foot, the faster it goes, up to its full speed at a whole lift, when a rope takes as long as the grown-ups chose. Once it's going, the foot has to come down under 25% of a whole lift to count as down again, so a foot that dips a little doesn't stop the walker.

**How the lifted foot is read** ([games/rooftop-rope/walk.js](games/rooftop-rope/walk.js), `liftOf` and `Lifter`). Only how much higher one ankle is than the other is read, as a share of the body's height, from the lower ankle up to the nose. So it doesn't matter how far from the camera the child stands. Under 1.2% of the height counts as on the floor, which is about how much the tracker's feet wobble. Ankles and a nose the tracker isn't sure of aren't used. A jump of more than 22% of the height between two looks is taken as the tracker's mistake, unless it's still there two looks later. With no feet to see for half a second, the lifted foot comes down by itself, gently. However the child's feet are while they stand still for the camera, and again at "Go!", is taken as level, unless one foot is more than 3.5% of the height above the other, so a child who stands with one foot a little in front of the other, which the camera sees as lower, starts from there. Standing still on a rooftop with both feet down, however they are then is taken as level again, once a rooftop, so a child who shuffles between ropes starts each one from where they stand. A foot held up, however low, is never taken as level.

**A foot on the floor behind the other is not a lifted foot,** though the camera sees it a little higher in the picture, as it's further off. On the test footage, with the camera at about hip height and three metres off, a foot 20 centimetres behind the other, a seventh of that person's height, reads as on the floor, and one 30 centimetres behind reads as 3% of their height: about a sixth of what a foot lifted that high reads as. So a foot 20 centimetres behind never moves the walker, at any setting, and one 25 centimetres behind doesn't with the usual setting. But a foot put down a long step behind can be taken for one just off the floor when a whole lift is very small, and nothing in what the tracker reports tells the two apart: its knee and its depth were both tried. So the child should start with the feet side by side, which the grown-ups' panel says.

**A short warm-up comes first,** before the first game. The character shows the move, and the child lifts one foot as high as they comfortably can and holds it, and then the other. The foot of the side being worked on comes first. A lift counts as high as it was held for 0.8 seconds, so a kick isn't taken for it, and a foot is done once it comes back down under 45% of that, or after 9 seconds. A whole lift with each foot is then 85% of what the child held with that foot, but never more than the grown-ups' setting, and never under 3% of the child's height. Less than 3% isn't taken as a measured lift. A foot that wasn't measured, and both feet when the warm-up is skipped with **Skip the warm-up**, ask for a whole lift of 7%, or the grown-ups' setting if that's smaller. The warm-up isn't repeated on the next game unless a grown-up has the body check done again.

**The kind rules.**

- **Resting on a rooftop costs nothing,** for as long as the child likes. After 6 seconds there, the child is told "Take your time. I'll wait for you."
- **A fall is a slow drop into a net,** with a bounce, and only that rope starts again. The stars and balloons the walker already has on it stay had. The walker falls after 9 seconds of standing on the rope with both feet down, or 5, or never, by the grown-ups' setting. Balancing again takes the wobble away twice as fast as it came. With "never" chosen, the walker waits on the rope for as long as it takes.
- **Nobody is stuck on a rope.** After two drops into the net from the same rope, the walker doesn't fall from that rope again: it waits on it for as long as it takes, and every lift, however short, takes it a little further. So a child who can only hold a foot up for a moment still gets across.
- **The other foot up,** on a rope that's for this one, doesn't move the walker, and doesn't wobble it either: the child is balancing, just not on the leg that was asked for. It steadies the walker as the asked foot would, and the child is told "Now the other foot!".
- **The ropes come down to meet a child who's trying.** A foot off the floor, at least 12% of the way to a whole lift but not yet at 40%, for 3 seconds, has a whole lift with that foot come down by 15%, never under 3% of the child's height, and the finish card says so.
- **With nobody in the picture,** for 0.6 seconds, everything waits, and the child is asked to come back. With the child there but their feet out of the picture, as when they've come too near the camera, they're asked to step back so their feet can be seen, and the walker waits without wobbling.
- **Nothing flashes.** A fall is a slow drop, and the camera stays up by the rope and looks down after the walker, rather than following it down.

**The grown-ups' settings,** under the gear icon:

- **Which foot lifts:** taking turns, one rope on each leg (the default); always standing on the leg being worked on, lifting the other foot; always lifting the foot of the leg being worked on; or whichever foot the child likes. The side being worked on is chosen in the same panel, and is the same in every game.
- **How high a whole lift is, at most:** just off the floor (4% of the child's height), a little (7%), medium (11%, about half way up the shin, the default) or high (17%, towards the knee). It's never more than 85% of the child's own lift with that foot from the warm-up.
- **How long a rope takes** with the foot at a whole lift, at level 1: 3, 5 (the default) or 8 seconds.
- **Ropes in a game:** 4, 6 (the default) or 8.
- **How soon the walker falls,** standing on the rope with both feet down: after 5 seconds, after 9 (the default), or never.
- **With friends:** together or against each other.

**Levels** ([games/rooftop-rope/walk.js](games/rooftop-rope/walk.js), `walkLevel` and `NEW_THINGS`). Level 1 is the plain rope. The ropes take a little longer as the levels go up, up to 1.3 times as long by level 10, and nothing gets quicker, so there's as long to get a foot up at level 10 as at level 1. The sky changes as the levels go up: morning at levels 1 to 3, the middle of the day at 4 to 6, the afternoon at 7 to 9, and sunset at 10. Two of the walker's friends wait on the last rooftop, and cheer as the walker gets there. On top of that, every level from 2 brings something new, which stays for the levels after it:

| Level | What's new |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Gold stars:** three along every rope, and four on the long one. |
| 3 | **Balloons** over the rope. A foot held higher than a whole lift, 1.2 times, pops them as the walker goes under, and "Higher!" is asked for just before one. Missing one costs nothing. |
| 4 | **A little bird** lands on the walker's head and rides across for as long as the foot stays up. A foot down for more than about a third of a second and it flies off. Carried to the end, it leaves a feather. |
| 5 | **A flag half way,** where the legs change. The walker waits at the flag until the other foot is up. There's no flag when the child lifts whichever foot they like. |
| 6 | **A crossing light half way,** red for 3 seconds: foot down and rest. Standing there doesn't wobble, and a foot put down there is counted as a rest well taken. |
| 7 | **A rope that climbs** to a much higher rooftop. A foot only just up hardly moves the walker, so it wants a high lift. |
| 8 | **The long rope,** twice as long, the one before last, with a round post half way to put the foot down on and rest. A foot put down a little before the post, or up to a seventh of the rope past it, steps onto it, as a child sees the walker get there before putting the foot down. |

| 10 | **The cup,** on the last rooftop, which is always the highest. |

The level's own new thing is on the first rope, so it's met right away, and again on a rope about half way through. Things from earlier levels are on some of the other ropes, one to a rope. The first time the level's new thing comes along, a small label at the top names it for a moment, in the language the voice speaks, and nothing new is said out loud. Which foot lifts, how high, how long a rope takes, how many ropes and how soon the walker falls stay as a grown-up set them.

## Sound

**A line is never cut off,** in any of the games, as nothing sounds more robotic. If something is being said, a new line waits for it to finish, after a short pause, and only the newest one waits, so a queue can't build up. A line that has waited more than 4 seconds is out of date and goes unsaid, and small remarks are simply dropped if anything's being said. Lines only stop mid-way if everything is stopping, such as when the panel opens.

**The English voices** are rendered ahead of time with [Kokoro](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M) on this computer, and the games just play the files. The coach is `af_heart`, and Mighty Moves also has an announcer and a voice for each fighter. To change what's said, edit a `lines.json` ([core/audio/voice/](core/audio/voice/lines.json) or [the game's own](games/mighty-moves/audio/voice/lines.json)) and run `npm run voice`. Rendering needs the Kokoro install from the `explainer-video` skill. Playing doesn't.

**The Georgian voices** are Microsoft's neural voices: Eka as the coach, and Giorgi as Mighty Moves' announcer, with pitch shifted so the fighters don't all sound alike. [tools/render_voice_ka.py](tools/render_voice_ka.py) renders them through the open-source `edge-tts` tool, which sends only the text of the lines. Choose **Voice language** under the gear icon. Spoken prompts then appear in Georgian too, as do Mighty Moves' move names and big words, though the rest of the screen stays in English for now.

**How the voice was chosen.** Since nobody here could listen, each candidate's clips went through Georgian speech recognizers, to see how much they could make out. On ten of the lines, with lower being better, one recognizer scored Giorgi 0.59, Eka 0.61, a Georgian voice model running on this computer 0.65, and Piper's Natia, the earlier stand-in, 0.84. That recognizer drops syllables on short clips, so only the comparison means much. A stronger one, Meta's Omnilingual, got Eka's lines 97% right and Natia's 93%, with most of the remaining errors being very short clips it heard nothing in. Microsoft's two also had by far the cleanest background. This shows the words are clear, but not that they sound natural or friendly, which needs a person to listen.

**To use a real person's voice instead,** record each line in `lines.json` as its own file named after its `id`, such as `open-right-hand.m4a`. A phone's voice memos are fine. Then run:

```
npm run voice:import -- <folder of recordings> ka
```

Each recording is trimmed and set to the same level as the rest. Any lines not recorded yet keep their current voice, so it can be done in parts.

**The car** is a real engine recording, played the way racing games do it: the pitch comes from a modelled rev counter with three gears, so it rises and drops through shifts. It's louder and brighter under power and falls away when coasting, so opening the hand gets an immediate rev as a reward. Tyre and wind noise builds with speed. ## Privacy

Camera frames are only ever used in the browser and are never saved. Each page's security policy blocks it from sending anything to another computer, except Google Analytics, and except that High Five Racers, Sky Wings, Balloon Pop, Mighty Moves, Bounce Back, Leap Trail, Giraffe's Maze, Cannon Bay, Tank Patrol, the Smoothie Stand, Spider Catcher, the Lighthouse Keeper, Giraffe's Garden, Frost Arena, Arrow Meadow, Giraffe's Puzzles, Flap, Little Chick!, Mighty Lift, Gobble and Grow, Giraffe's Paint Box, Moto Mountain and Rooftop Rope may reach the server for playing with friends, which they only do once a grown-up makes or joins a race, a flight, a game, a fight, a run, a battle, a match, a flock, a ride or a crossing (see [Racing friends](#high-five-racers)), and the camera turns off as soon as a round ends. Only settings are stored, in the browser, along with which level each game is on. A round's numbers are shown once and then discarded. All libraries, the model and the sounds are in this folder, so the games also work offline.

Gobble and Grow reads the face rather than the hands, in the same way. Its tracker runs in the browser, and all that comes out of it is where the points of the face are, which are used there and then, to say how open the mouth is and where the face is, and never kept or sent. Nothing recognises anyone. With friends it reaches the same server as the other games, on the same terms, and only once a grown-up makes or joins a game.

**Google Analytics** (`G-BPC62S5T34`) counts visits to every page, through [core/analytics.js](core/analytics.js): Google's tag says which page was opened, with what any website's visit tells it, such as the browser, the screen size and roughly where. Nothing from this site goes with it: never anything from the camera, and nothing about how a child plays. Google signals and ad personalisation are turned off, as this is a site for children. It sets Google's own cookies, which in some countries means a website should ask first. It never loads on this computer, as in the tests, so nothing leaves it there. Each page's security policy lets in Google's tag and analytics, and nothing else new.

## Tests

```
npm install     # once, for Playwright
npm test
```

Unit tests run the rules against what the real model reported for real photos and videos. The Playwright tests run the real games, trackers and models in your installed Chrome, with the webcam replaced by a canvas showing photos of hands, or footage of a person for the whole-body games ([test/e2e/fake-camera.js](test/e2e/fake-camera.js)). They write screenshots to `test-results/screens/`.

[tools/record_pose.mjs](tools/record_pose.mjs) records what the model reports for a video, which is where [test/fixtures/body-moves.json](test/fixtures/body-moves.json) came from. What happens in each clip was checked by eye against its frames.

There's no free footage of a person clapping, circling a hand or holding their arms out in full view of a camera, so [tools/render_test_moves.py](tools/render_test_moves.py) renders it in Blender with a rigged character. The real tracker reads those pictures, so what's made up is the person, not the tracking. Those three moves have only been tried on that rendered person. The same goes for raising an arm, in Mighty Lift, and for leaning to the side, in Moto Mountain: its footage ([test/fixtures/body/leans.webm](test/fixtures/body/leans.webm)) is a person bending sideways at the waist, first evenly from 20 degrees one way to 20 the other, so that a test can pick any bend by its moment in the film, and then leaning to each side and holding it, as in the warm-up. What the tracker reported for it is kept in [test/fixtures/moto-mountain-leans.json](test/fixtures/moto-mountain-leans.json) for the unit tests. The same goes for standing on one leg, in Rooftop Rope: its footage ([test/fixtures/body/stands.webm](test/fixtures/body/stands.webm)) is a person lifting each foot in turn, evenly from the floor to 48 centimetres, so that a test can pick any height by its moment in the film, and then lifting each foot 30 centimetres and holding it, as in the warm-up, and the right one just 8 centimetres. What the tracker reported for it is kept in [test/fixtures/rooftop-rope-stands.json](test/fixtures/rooftop-rope-stands.json). A second film ([test/fixtures/body/staggers.webm](test/fixtures/body/staggers.webm), and [test/fixtures/rooftop-rope-staggers.json](test/fixtures/rooftop-rope-staggers.json)) is the same person with both feet on the floor, sliding one and then the other back to 40 centimetres behind, which is not a lifted foot, for the tests that a foot put down behind doesn't move the walker. The test camera can also tip any footage over about the person's feet (`tiltFootage` in [test/e2e/fake-camera.js](test/e2e/fake-camera.js)), which makes a person standing still look like one leaning all in a piece, by exactly as many degrees as a test asks for.

**The games played with the face** can't be tested with photos, as there are no photos of faces in this folder, and none may be added. The unit tests read made-up faces ([test/fixtures/made-up-face.js](test/fixtures/made-up-face.js)): the points the tracker would report, with the mouth as far open as each test says, and the head turned, tipped, far off or half out of the picture. In the Playwright tests the webcam is a drawing of a face, and the tracker a stand-in that reports that same face ([test/e2e/fake-face.js](test/e2e/fake-face.js)), which the game only takes when its page is opened for testing. Everything after the tracker is the real thing. The real tracker and its model are tested for loading, running on the camera's frames, falling back to the CPU, and seeing nobody in an empty room. They were also run through the whole game once, by hand, on a painting kept outside this folder: see [How the mouth is read](#how-the-mouth-is-read).

## Not tested yet

- **Children's hands, and hands with hemiplegia.** Everything so far uses photos of adult hands. A flexed wrist, a pronated forearm or a very small hand could all read differently. This is the most important thing to try next, and the panel's live finger bars are there to help with it.
- **Children's bodies, and bodies with hemiplegia, in the whole-body games.** The move rules were tuned on videos of fit adults, doing karate and workouts, and on a rendered character for claps, hand circles and arms held out as wings. A child's moves may be slower and smaller, and an affected side may move very differently. *How big a movement has to be* is the setting to try first.
- **How the games feel.** Rules, timing and every screen are tested, but only a child can say whether Leap Trail's speeds are right, whether its step to the side is the right size and lanes are easy to find, which was only tried on adult footage moved across the picture, whether the films are clear enough to copy, whether Mighty Moves' pace works, how tiring it is to hold both arms out in Sky Wings, and whether any of it is fun for more than a few minutes.

- **Reading a moving hand at a distance.** Hands were only tested on sharp photos shrunk to size. A hand in the middle of a strike will be blurrier, so fewer strikes may be read than that test suggests, and a real webcam's picture may matter a lot.

Flying with friends was only tried with two browsers on one computer, and with two games wired together in the unit tests, never with three or four in a browser, or over the internet. Nobody has listened to its sound, it has no animal noises, and its Georgian lines and labels need a native speaker to check them.

How high a line is, how long a hold should be, and what counts as putting a weight down gently (half a second) or holding it steady were tuned on that one rendered person, with about twice that as a margin. Whether a child with hemiplegia can hold the arm up for the hold, whether an affected arm raised with the shoulder hitched reads as high as it looks, and whether the line coming down to meet them comes soon enough need real children to say. Playing with friends was only tried with two browsers on one computer, nobody has listened to its sound, and its Georgian lines and labels need a native speaker to check them.
- **How Giraffe's Paint Box feels.** Whether keeping a hand open while it moves across the picture is clear and not too tiring for a young child, whether the brush is big enough and the parts fill in soon enough for an affected hand, whether the dots and pots make it clear which paint a part wants, and whether the levels' new things are fun or a distraction. It was only played with photos of an adult hand moved about by the tests, with some wobble. Nobody has listened to its sound, playing with friends was only tried with two browsers on one computer, and its Georgian lines and labels need a native speaker to check them.
- **The fox that can catch, in Flap, Little Chick!.** He has only caught chicks flown by scripted hands. Whether three seconds at a thing, and about eleven more as he comes on, is fair for a child with hemiplegia who needs both hands up for a fence, isn't known: it's set by how long a rest should be, not by anyone's hands. If it's too hard, **The fox** under the gear icon has him as he was.

A grown-up tried the first version with their own body and found the bike's movement too drastic, so how a lean turns the bike was changed (see "How a lean turns the bike"), and nobody has tried the new way with a real body yet. The lean has only been read, by the real tracker, from two rendered people: one standing still and tipped over by the test camera, as a body leaning all in a piece, and one bending sideways at the waist ([test/fixtures/body/leans.webm](test/fixtures/body/leans.webm)). Whether 85% of the warm-up's lean is the right whole lean, whether a little over half of it is the right ask for a bend, whether three seconds of trying is soon enough for the bends to come down to meet a child, and how tiring it is to hold a lean for a long bend, are for a therapist and a child to say.
- **The wild ride, in Moto Mountain.** The race, the hills, the hay bales, the river and the tricks have only been ridden by pretend children, in the rules' own tests and in the browser. Whether the other riders are a fair race for a real child, or for a grown-up, whether a lean in the air and upright again in a second and a half is something a child with hemiplegia can do towards their weaker side, whether steering round bales is possible with a real body's lag, and whether flying off the hills is fun or too much, aren't known. The riders aren't slowed by puddles, sheep, bridges or scooters, which only the child's bike meets, so a level with more of those is a harder race.

So how a real child's lift reads, whether a chair or a grown-up in front of the legs hides the feet from the tracker, and how steady the feet are on a real webcam, aren't known. A foot put down a long step behind the other can be read as just off the floor when a whole lift is very small, as said above, and how often a real child finds that out isn't known either: only a foot lifted with the knee coming forward was filmed, not one bent up behind or held out to the side. Whether 85% of the warm-up's lift is the right whole lift, whether 9 seconds before a fall is kind enough, whether three seconds of trying is soon enough for a lift to come down, and how long a child with hemiplegia can stand on the weaker leg, are for a therapist and a child to say.

Three fingers and the grown-up's own choice of fingers have only been tested on the finger scores, and the new Georgian lines need a native speaker.
- **Flying with the other hand, on a real child.** It was tested with photos of a fist and an open hand. A hand with hemiplegia might not read as a clear fist, and nobody has checked whether reaching out the second hand pulls the hand in play out of view.
- **Kicks seen from the front.** The only kick footage available was filmed from the side, which confuses the tracker's left and right legs, so kicks were tested with a real standing body that had its legs moved by the test.
- **A real webcam.** Only simulated camera feeds have been used. Real lighting, backgrounds and motion may need some tuning. That goes double for Balloon Pop: how hard balloons are to reach, and how it feels to chase them, can only be judged with a real hand moving.
- **The left and right label on these hands.** It has only been checked on photos of adult hands, so a child's hand or a hand with hemiplegia could be named wrongly. If the tracker is confidently wrong, the hand won't count, and the panel says "Only sees the other hand", so it's quick to spot.
- **How it all sounds.** Loading, levels, clipping and timing are tested and the mix was measured, but the sound choices, the music and the balance need a human ear. The Georgian especially needs a native speaker's, both the voices and my translations.
- **Real phones and tablets.** The layouts were checked at those sizes in desktop Chrome.
- **A real mouth, in Gobble and Grow.** No real face has played it. ## Known limits

- **Space.** Mighty Moves needs the child's whole body in view, which takes about 2 to 3 metres from the camera, and it needs a clear floor. Sky Wings needs room for both arms out to the sides. They can't tell if there's something nearby to bump into.
- **A circle has to be drawn facing the camera.** Hand circles are read from the picture, so an arm going round in line with the camera, from front to back, looks like a line and doesn't count. About two thirds of the way round is enough, as a hand crossing in front of the body is often lost for a moment. A hand's wrist turning on the spot isn't read either.
- **A clap can't be heard.** It's read from the hands coming together, so hands meeting silently count too.
- **Distance.** In the hand games, the tracker only finds a new hand when the palm is at least about 15% of the picture's height. Once found, it follows the hand down to about 6%. For a child's hand on a typical webcam, that works out at roughly 60 cm and 1.5 m from the camera. Those distances are calculated rather than measured. The hand check shows how big hands need to look.
- **A hand hidden behind the hand in play can't be seen.** The camera only sees what's in front, so a child could hold the hand open from behind. An adult watching will notice, but the game won't.
- **The wrist isn't measured.** Hand tracking can't see the forearm, so it can't tell if a child opens their fingers by bending the wrist down, a common way of compensating. A therapist may want to watch for this. Giraffe's Goal goes part of the way: it can tell a hand that's tipped over towards the camera or hanging down, which is how a dropped wrist looks with the arm held up, but not how far the wrist itself is bent. Flap, Little Chick! reads the same two things for each hand, as a rise from tipped forwards to held up, and can't tell either whether it was the wrist or the forearm that brought the hand up.
- **The wrist isn't measured.** Hand tracking can't see the forearm, so it can't tell if a child opens their fingers by bending the wrist down, a common way of compensating. A therapist may want to watch for this. Giraffe's Goal goes part of the way: it can tell a hand that's tipped over towards the camera or hanging down, which is how a dropped wrist looks with the arm held up, but not how far the wrist itself is bent.
- **How close the face has to be.** The face tracker only finds a new face when it's about a quarter of the picture's height or more, and can follow it a little further than that. For a child's face on a typical webcam, that works out at within about 90 cm of the camera, which is calculated rather than measured. Further away, Gobble and Grow asks the child to come closer.
- **One side of the mouth isn't told from the other.** How open a mouth is, is measured in the middle of the lips. A mouth that opens more on one side, as with a weak side of the face, is read by its middle, and the game can't tell whether both sides opened. A therapist may want to watch for it.

These games are not medical devices and don't replace therapy. Please check targets and how long to play with the child's therapist.
